# Language Comparison Policy

Shared policy for comparing a learner's support language with the target
language.

The purpose of comparison is to improve understanding, not to force one
language's grammar onto another.

---

## 1. Core Principle

Use language comparison as a bridge, not as a rule source.

Prefer:

**"This resembles..."**

over:

**"This works exactly like..."**

unless the systems are genuinely equivalent.

---

## 2. Comparison Goals

Use comparison when it helps the learner:

- understand an unfamiliar grammatical concept,
- notice a structural difference,
- avoid direct translation,
- recognize a false friend,
- understand word order,
- understand morphology,
- remember a new pattern.

Do not compare languages merely because both are known to the learner.

---

## 3. Avoid False Equivalence

Do not assume that similar labels mean identical grammatical behavior.

Examples include:

- tense,
- aspect,
- grammatical gender,
- articles,
- case,
- subject,
- object,
- reflexive forms,
- politeness,
- perfect constructions.

Two languages may use the same grammatical term while implementing it
differently.

---

## 4. Source-Language Transfer

Watch for errors caused by transferring patterns from the learner's support
language.

Possible transfer areas include:

- word order,
- article usage,
- pronouns,
- tense,
- prepositions,
- grammatical gender,
- plural formation,
- politeness,
- pronunciation,
- collocations.

When transfer occurs:

1. identify the transferred pattern,
2. explain the target-language difference,
3. provide a reusable contrast,
4. ask for a short production task.

---

## 5. Positive Transfer

Comparison should also identify useful similarities.

If the learner's known language provides a reliable bridge, use it.

Examples may include:

- cognates,
- similar conjugation concepts,
- grammatical gender,
- formal vs informal address,
- shared word order patterns.

Make clear where the similarity ends.

---

## 6. False Friends

When two languages contain visually or phonetically similar words with different
meanings, mark them clearly.

Prefer teaching false friends through contextual examples rather than isolated
warnings.

---

## 7. Terminology

Use grammatical terminology from another language only when it genuinely helps.

For example, a learner familiar with German may benefit from terms such as:

- grammatical gender,
- case,
- verb position,
- agreement.

However, do not imply that the target language uses the same system simply
because the terminology is familiar.

---

## 8. Translation Direction

Remember that language learning is directional.

A contrast useful for:

**Language A → Language B**

may need different emphasis for:

**Language B → Language A**

For example, one direction may focus on introducing a grammatical category,
while the reverse direction may focus on learning when that category is absent.

Shared contrastive facts may remain bidirectional, but teaching emphasis should
follow the learner's direction.

---

## 9. Support Language

Use the learner's designated support language for explanation when it improves
clarity.

Do not unnecessarily introduce a third comparison language unless:

- the learner already uses it,
- it provides a useful conceptual bridge,
- the comparison remains concise.

---

## 10. Multiple Known Languages

If the learner knows several languages, choose the comparison that provides the
clearest and least misleading explanation.

Do not stack several language comparisons into every answer.

Prefer one strong comparison over several weak ones.

---

## 11. Structural Comparison

Good comparison format:

> Target language:
> ...
>
> Support language:
> ...
>
> Main difference:
> ...

Keep the comparison focused on the specific pattern being learned.

---

## 12. Cultural and Pragmatic Differences

Language comparison may include differences in:

- politeness,
- forms of address,
- conversational norms,
- directness,
- register.

Do not reduce pragmatic differences to literal vocabulary equivalence.

A phrase that translates directly may carry a different social meaning.

---

## 13. Regional Variety

When either language has major regional varieties, compare the specific target
variety rather than treating the language as uniform.

Examples:

- European vs Brazilian Portuguese,
- regional German usage,
- English varieties,
- Chinese regional standards.

Do not mix regional systems without explanation.

---

## 14. Avoid Literal Translation Rules

Do not create rules such as:

> Word X always equals Word Y.

Prefer contextual equivalence.

Words and grammatical structures often map differently depending on:

- context,
- register,
- collocation,
- aspect,
- syntax,
- idiom.

---

## 15. Correction Strategy

When a learner error clearly results from source-language transfer:

1. first allow self-correction when reasonable,
2. point out the transferred pattern,
3. contrast it with the target pattern,
4. ask for one new example.

Avoid turning every correction into a long comparative grammar lecture.

---

## 16. Core Rule

Comparison is successful when it helps the learner form the correct
target-language pattern independently.

It should reduce confusion, not create a permanent dependency on translation.
