# Traditional Chinese ↔ German (Germany)

Shared contrastive reference for:

- Traditional Chinese (`zh-TW`)
- German as used in Germany (`de-DE`)

This file describes structural differences, useful similarities, common transfer
risks, and comparison strategies between the two languages.

It is bidirectional.

Direction-specific teaching behavior belongs in `skills/`.

CEFR-level difficulty belongs in `shared/cefr/`.

General correction and retrieval behavior belongs in `shared/pedagogy/`.

German language rules belong in `languages/de-DE.md`.

Traditional Chinese language rules belong in `languages/zh-TW.md`.

---

## 1. Core Principle

Use contrastive explanation to help the learner understand differences between
Traditional Chinese and German.

Do not force one language's grammar onto the other.

Prefer:

**identify difference → show contrast → produce target-language pattern**

over:

**literal translation → memorized equivalence**

---

## 2. Major Structural Contrast

Traditional Chinese and German differ substantially in:

- grammatical gender,
- case marking,
- articles,
- verb conjugation and agreement,
- tense and aspect marking,
- verb position and the verbal bracket,
- subordinate-clause word order,
- separable verbs,
- adjective endings,
- plural marking,
- compounds,
- prepositions and case government,
- politeness (`du` / `Sie`),
- writing system, capitalization, and pronunciation.

These differences make direct word-for-word translation unreliable.

---

## 3. Grammatical Gender

Traditional Chinese has no grammatical gender.

German nouns are masculine, feminine, or neuter, and the gender affects the
article, adjective ending, and pronoun.

> der Tisch, die Lampe, das Buch

Common transfer risk:

> learning the noun without its article.

Teach the noun with its article and plural.

---

## 4. Cases

Traditional Chinese does not mark grammatical case on nouns or pronouns. Roles
are expressed by word order, particles, and coverbs.

German marks case mainly on articles, pronouns, and adjectives.

> Der Mann sieht den Hund.

> Den Hund sieht der Mann.

In the second sentence, the case marking shows who does what even though the
order changed.

Common transfer risks:

- using the nominative form everywhere,
- relying on word order alone to show roles,
- confusing accusative and dative.

Do not map a German case to a Chinese particle. Teach the function and the
forms in high-frequency phrases.

---

## 5. Articles

Traditional Chinese has no article system comparable to German.

Common transfer errors include:

- omitting the article,
- using the wrong gender or case,
- treating `der / die / das` as interchangeable,
- treating `ein` as a numeral `一` in every context.

Do not explain articles merely as equivalents of Chinese demonstratives such as
`這` and `那`.

---

## 6. Verb Conjugation

Chinese verbs do not conjugate for person.

> 我住 / 你住 / 他住

German verbs do.

> ich wohne, du wohnst, er wohnt

Common transfer risks:

- using the infinitive for every subject,
- omitting the ending because the pronoun is present,
- forgetting the stem-vowel change (`du fährst`, `er liest`),
- misusing `sein` and `haben`.

---

## 7. Tense and Aspect

Chinese marks time mainly through time words, context, and aspect particles
such as `了`, `過`, and `著`.

German marks tense on the verb. The present tense is often used for the future
with a time expression, and the perfect (*Perfekt*) is the normal past in
speech.

Avoid one-to-one mappings such as:

> 了 = Perfekt

A learner may under-mark past time or use the present tense where a past form
is needed.

The German choice between `haben` and `sein` in the perfect has no counterpart
in Chinese and should be taught with the verb.

---

## 8. Verb Position and the Verbal Bracket

Chinese is largely SVO, with modifiers and adverbials placed before the verb.

German main clauses put the finite verb in second position, and the other verb
part at the end.

> Ich habe gestern einen Film gesehen.

> 我昨天看了一部電影。

Chinese places the time word before the verb, as German often does, but the
German participle goes to the end of the clause, which has no Chinese
counterpart.

Common transfer risks:

- leaving the participle or infinitive next to the finite verb,
- placing the verb third after a fronted time expression
  (*Gestern ich habe…*),
- forgetting the separable prefix at the end.

Teach the pattern with short, repeated frames.

---

## 9. Subordinate Clauses

Chinese subordinate clauses generally precede the main clause and use
connectors such as `因為` or `如果`, without changing verb position.

German subordinate clauses send the finite verb to the end.

> Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Berlin arbeiten möchte.

Common transfer risks:

- keeping main-clause order after `weil`, `dass`, or `wenn`,
- placing the subordinate clause first without inverting the main clause
  (*Wenn ich Zeit habe, ich komme.*),
- treating `weil` and `denn` as identical.

When a subordinate clause comes first, the main clause verb follows it
immediately:

> Wenn ich Zeit habe, komme ich.

---

## 10. Relative Clauses and Modification

Chinese modifiers, including relative clauses, precede the noun and usually use
`的`.

> 我昨天買的書

German relative clauses follow the noun and have a relative pronoun.

> das Buch, das ich gestern gekauft habe

Do not transfer Chinese pre-nominal order. Note that the German relative
pronoun cannot be omitted.

German does allow a long participial phrase before a noun in written language.
Treat it as an advanced structure and do not teach it early.

---

## 11. Adjective Endings

Chinese adjectives do not change.

German adjectives before a noun take endings by gender, number, and case.

> ein großer Mann, eine große Frau, ein großes Kind

An adjective after `sein` has no ending.

Common transfer risks:

- omitting endings,
- using one ending for all cases,
- adding an ending after `sein`.

Introduce endings gradually.

---

## 12. Plural Marking

Chinese seldom marks plurals on nouns and relies on numbers, classifiers, or
context.

German marks plurals on the noun and the article, with several patterns.

> ein Buch, zwei Bücher

Common transfer risks:

- using the singular after a number,
- applying one plural ending to every noun,
- forgetting that `die` is the plural article for all genders.

---

## 13. Classifiers and Measure Words

Chinese uses classifiers such as `個`, `本`, `杯`, and `張`.

German has no general classifier system.

> zwei Bücher (not *zwei Stück Buch)

For units, German uses a noun without a plural for many measure words:

> zwei Glas Wasser, drei Kilo Äpfel

Do not search for a German equivalent of every Chinese classifier.

---

## 14. Separable Verbs

Chinese has verb–complement structures, but not separable prefixes that move to
the end of a clause.

> Ich rufe dich an.

Common transfer risks:

- keeping the prefix attached to the verb in a main clause,
- forgetting the prefix,
- separating an inseparable verb.

Teach separable verbs as a chunk, together with a full sentence.

---

## 15. Modal Verbs

Chinese modals (`可以`, `要`, `會`, `能`, `應該`) precede the main verb.

German modals do as well, but the main verb goes to the end as an infinitive.

> Ich muss heute arbeiten.

Mapping is not one-to-one:

- `可以` may correspond to `können` or `dürfen`,
- `要` may correspond to `wollen`, `müssen`, or `werden`,
- `不用` corresponds to `nicht müssen`, not to `nicht dürfen`.

Do not teach a modal by a single Chinese gloss.

---

## 16. Negation

Chinese uses `不`, `沒`, and `沒有`.

German uses `nicht` and `kein`.

> Ich habe keinen Hund.

> Ich kenne ihn nicht.

Common transfer risk:

> using `nicht` where `kein` is needed, or the reverse.

`nicht` also has a variable position, unlike Chinese `不`, which precedes the
verb.

---

## 17. Questions

Chinese forms questions with particles such as `嗎` and `呢`, question words in
place, A-not-A patterns, or context.

German yes/no questions put the verb first, and wh-questions put the question
word first.

> Kommst du morgen?

> Wann kommst du?

Common transfer risks:

- forming a question with intonation only,
- keeping the question word in the position of the answer,
- omitting inversion after the question word.

---

## 18. Subject Omission

Chinese often omits subjects when the context makes them clear.

German normally requires a subject.

> Ich weiß es nicht.

Common transfer risk:

> dropping the subject or the dummy `es`.

Teach `es` in weather, time, and impersonal expressions:

> Es regnet. Es gibt einen Bus.

---

## 19. Copula and Adjectival Predicates

Chinese adjectives can act as predicates without a copula.

> 他很高。

German requires `sein`.

> Er ist groß.

Chinese `是` should not be mapped mechanically onto `sein`. German also uses
`sein` in many places where Chinese uses other structures.

---

## 20. `haben`, `es gibt`, and Existence

Chinese `有` covers possession and existence.

German separates these:

> Ich habe einen Bruder.

> Es gibt hier ein Café.

Do not teach `有 = haben` as a complete rule.

---

## 21. Prepositions and Case Government

Chinese expresses relations with coverbs, localizers, and word order.

> 在桌子上

German prepositions govern a case, and two-way prepositions distinguish
location from movement.

> Das Buch liegt auf dem Tisch. (dative, location)

> Ich lege das Buch auf den Tisch. (accusative, movement)

Direct translation of a preposition is unreliable. Teach prepositions in
phrases, together with the case.

---

## 22. Compounds

Chinese also forms compounds, which can provide positive transfer.

> 手機, 火車站

German compounds are written as one word, and the last element determines the
gender.

> das Handy, der Bahnhof, die Bahnhofsuhr

Encourage the learner to break a compound into parts when reading. Do not
assume that a German compound maps onto a Chinese compound character by
character.

---

## 23. Forms of Address

Chinese may use `您`, titles, kinship terms, role nouns, or omission.

German uses `du` and `Sie`, with a choice of verb form and pronoun.

Do not map:

> 您 = Sie

as a universal equivalence.

Register depends on the relationship and the setting. Teach `Sie` as the safe
default for strangers and formal contexts.

---

## 24. Writing System, Capitalization, and Spelling

Chinese uses a morphosyllabic script with no capital letters.

German uses the Latin alphabet with `ä`, `ö`, `ü`, and `ß`, and capitalizes
**all nouns**.

> Ich habe einen Hund.

Common transfer risks:

- not capitalizing nouns,
- overusing capitals for other words,
- omitting umlauts,
- long words being misread as several words.

German spelling is more regular than English spelling, and this can be used as a
positive transfer point.

---

## 25. Pronunciation: Segments

Chinese and German share some sounds and differ in others.

Areas that may need attention:

- `ü` and `ö`: `ü` resembles Mandarin `ü`, which is a helpful positive
  transfer, while `ö` has no direct equivalent,
- the `ch` sounds `[ç]` and `[x]`, and `r`,
- `pf` and `z` [ts],
- `sp` and `st` at the start of a word (pronounced `[ʃp]`, `[ʃt]`),
- final devoicing (`Tag` /taːk/),
- consonant clusters, which Mandarin does not allow (`Strumpf`),
- vowel length.

Avoid misleading transliterations using Chinese characters.

Prefer:

- audio,
- articulatory explanation,
- IPA when useful,
- approximate comparison only when clearly labeled.

---

## 26. Pronunciation: Tone, Stress, and Rhythm

Mandarin uses lexical tones. German does not.

A Chinese-speaking learner may:

- apply Mandarin tone patterns to German syllables,
- pronounce every syllable with equal weight,
- avoid word-final consonant clusters.

Word stress and sentence intonation in German are not tonal. Teach stress on
the first syllable of many native words, and the reduction of `-e`, `-en`, and
`-er`.

Do not treat German intonation as a set of tones.

---

## 27. Positive Transfer Through English

Many zh-TW learners already know English.

English and German share cognates, some grammar, and the Latin alphabet:

> Haus / house, Hand / hand, Wasser / water

English can help with cognates, present perfect vs. Perfekt, and modal verbs.

English can also mislead:

- false friends (`bekommen` = get, `Gift` = poison),
- verb position,
- case and gender, which English lacks.

Use English comparisons only when the learner benefits, and never require
English for understanding.

---

## 28. Common zh-TW → de-DE Transfer Risks

Common risks include:

- omitting the article or using the wrong gender,
- using the wrong case,
- not conjugating the verb,
- wrong verb position (verb not second, or not at the end of a subordinate
  clause),
- participle or infinitive not at the end,
- separable prefix not separated,
- singular noun after a number,
- confusing `nicht` and `kein`,
- dropping the subject or `es`,
- omitting `sein`,
- mapping `有` onto `haben` in existential sentences,
- Chinese pre-nominal relative-clause order,
- lowercase nouns,
- using `du` and `Sie` inappropriately.

Use these as diagnostic possibilities, not assumptions about every learner.

---

## 29. Common de-DE → zh-TW Transfer Risks

For German-speaking learners of Traditional Chinese, possible transfer risks
include:

- adding grammatical gender or case to nouns and pronouns,
- over-marking tense,
- classifier omission,
- incorrect `的` usage,
- German-style relative-clause order,
- placing the verb at the end or in second position,
- unnatural politeness mapping,
- overuse of explicit subjects.

These belong to the same language pair but should be emphasized only when the
learning direction is de-DE → zh-TW.

---

## 30. Contrastive Explanation Format

When comparison is useful, prefer a compact structure:

> **de-DE:**  
> Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt.
>
> **zh-TW:**  
> 我二十歲。
>
> **差異：**  
> 德語用 `sein` 加 `alt`，並保留 `Jahre`；中文不需要對應的動詞。

Avoid long comparative lectures unless the learner requests deeper analysis.

---

## 31. Translation Policy

Do not assume a one-to-one mapping between words or structures.

Prefer translating:

- meaning,
- grammatical function,
- register,
- natural usage.

When literal translation is pedagogically useful, label it clearly as literal.

Distinguish:

- literal translation,
- natural translation,
- structural explanation.

## 32. Bidirectional Use

This file is intentionally bidirectional.

For:

**zh-TW → de-DE**

emphasize German structures that are absent or substantially different in
Chinese.

For:

**de-DE → zh-TW**

emphasize Chinese structures that are absent or substantially different in
German.

Do not duplicate stable contrastive facts in separate directional files.

---

## 33. Core Principle

Use the differences between Traditional Chinese and German to predict useful
teaching moments, not to stereotype learner errors.

Prefer:

**contrast → awareness → target-language production**

over:

**literal equivalence → memorization → transfer error**
