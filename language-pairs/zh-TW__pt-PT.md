# Traditional Chinese ↔ European Portuguese

Shared contrastive reference for:

- Traditional Chinese (`zh-TW`)
- European Portuguese (`pt-PT`)

This file describes structural differences, useful similarities, common transfer
risks, and comparison strategies between the two languages.

It is bidirectional.

Direction-specific teaching behavior belongs in `skills/`.

CEFR-level difficulty belongs in `shared/cefr/`.

General correction and retrieval behavior belongs in `shared/pedagogy/`.

European Portuguese language rules belong in `languages/pt-PT.md`.

---

## 1. Core Principle

Use contrastive explanation to help the learner understand differences between
Traditional Chinese and European Portuguese.

Do not force one language's grammar onto the other.

Prefer:

**identify difference → show contrast → produce target-language pattern**

over:

**literal translation → memorized equivalence**

---

## 2. Major Structural Contrast

Traditional Chinese and European Portuguese differ substantially in:

- grammatical gender,
- articles,
- verb conjugation,
- tense and aspect marking,
- pronoun systems,
- clitic placement,
- agreement,
- prepositions,
- number marking,
- word order flexibility,
- subject omission,
- politeness strategies,
- pronunciation and rhythm.

These differences make direct word-for-word translation unreliable.

---

## 3. Grammatical Gender

Traditional Chinese does not have grammatical gender comparable to Portuguese.

European Portuguese distinguishes masculine and feminine forms in many nouns,
articles, adjectives, possessives, and related structures.

Examples:

> o livro

> a casa

> um amigo português

> uma amiga portuguesa

For a zh-TW speaker learning pt-PT, gender should be learned together with the
noun whenever practical.

Prefer:

> a casa

over memorizing only:

> casa

Common transfer risk:

> treating the noun as independent from its article and gender pattern.

---

## 4. Articles

Traditional Chinese does not have a direct equivalent of the Portuguese
definite and indefinite article system.

Portuguese uses forms such as:

- o
- a
- os
- as
- um
- uma
- uns
- umas

This may initially feel optional to a Chinese-speaking learner.

Common transfer errors include:

- omitting articles,
- using the wrong gender,
- using the wrong number,
- failing to contract articles with prepositions.

Do not explain articles merely as direct equivalents of Chinese demonstratives.

---

## 5. Verb Conjugation

Traditional Chinese verbs do not conjugate for grammatical person in the same
way Portuguese verbs do.

Compare:

Chinese:

> 我住  
> 你住  
> 他住

The verb remains morphologically unchanged.

Portuguese:

> eu moro  
> tu moras  
> ele mora

For zh-TW → pt-PT learning, verb endings must therefore be treated as a new
grammatical system.

Common transfer risks:

- using the infinitive instead of a conjugated form,
- using one verb form for all subjects,
- relying too heavily on explicit subject pronouns instead of learning the verb
  ending.

---

## 6. Subject Pronouns

Both languages may omit subjects under some conditions, but the systems are not
the same.

Traditional Chinese frequently omits subjects when context makes them clear.

Portuguese is also a pro-drop language, but verb morphology often identifies the
subject.

Example:

> Moro em Lisboa.

The ending of `moro` indicates first-person singular.

Do not teach Portuguese subject omission as simply identical to Chinese topic
omission.

The mechanisms differ.

---

## 7. Tense and Aspect

Traditional Chinese does not use verb inflection for tense in the same way as
Portuguese.

Chinese often relies on:

- context,
- time expressions,
- aspect markers,
- particles.

Portuguese frequently encodes tense and aspect through verb morphology and
auxiliary constructions.

This difference can cause Chinese-speaking learners to under-mark tense in
Portuguese.

Avoid one-to-one mappings such as:

> 了 = Portuguese past tense

The systems are not equivalent.

---

## 8. `ser` and `estar`

Traditional Chinese does not have a direct grammatical distinction equivalent to
Portuguese `ser` and `estar`.

Chinese `是` must not be mapped mechanically to either verb.

Compare:

> Sou português.

> Estou cansado.

> Ela está em casa.

A Chinese-speaking learner may need to learn Portuguese copular and state
constructions as separate usage patterns.

Do not teach:

> `ser` = 是  
> `estar` = another version of 是

as a complete explanation.

---

## 9. `ter` and Age

Chinese commonly expresses age with structures such as:

> 我二十歲。

Portuguese uses `ter`:

> Tenho vinte anos.

Literal transfer can produce incorrect Portuguese.

Teach the expression as a reusable chunk:

> ter + number + anos

---

## 10. Agreement

Traditional Chinese generally does not require the same type of grammatical
gender and number agreement found in Portuguese.

Portuguese agreement may involve:

- articles,
- nouns,
- adjectives,
- possessives,
- demonstratives,
- participles in relevant constructions.

Example:

> a casa branca

> as casas brancas

Chinese-speaking learners may understand the meaning while overlooking the
agreement morphology.

When correcting, highlight the relationship between the words rather than each
ending in isolation.

---

## 11. Plural Marking

Chinese plural marking is much more limited and structurally different from
Portuguese.

Portuguese commonly marks plurality across multiple elements.

Example:

> os livros novos

A Chinese-speaking learner may initially mark plurality incompletely.

Possible transfer error:

> *os livro novo

Teach plural agreement as a phrase-level pattern.

---

## 12. Prepositions

Portuguese relies heavily on prepositions.

Traditional Chinese often expresses equivalent relationships using:

- coverbs,
- localizers,
- word order,
- context,
- other constructions.

Direct translation is therefore unreliable.

Common Portuguese difficulties may include:

- de
- em
- a
- para
- por
- com

Teach prepositions in phrases and constructions, not only as isolated vocabulary
equivalents.

---

## 13. Preposition + Article Contractions

Portuguese contractions have no close equivalent in Traditional Chinese.

Examples:

> de + o → do

> em + a → na

> a + o → ao

A Chinese-speaking learner may understand both components but fail to contract
them.

Treat frequent contractions as complete forms in early learning.

Examples:

> no Porto

> na escola

> do Brasil

> ao supermercado

---

## 14. Clitic Pronouns

European Portuguese clitic pronouns have no close structural equivalent in
Traditional Chinese.

Examples:

> Chamo-me Ana.

> Não me lembro.

> Como te chamas?

> Diz-lhe a verdade.

This can be one of the more unfamiliar areas for a Chinese-speaking learner.

Avoid explaining clitics only through literal Chinese translation.

Teach:

- the verb pattern,
- pronoun function,
- pronoun position,
- triggering environment,

as a combined structure.

---

## 15. Clitic Placement

Traditional Chinese does not have an enclisis/proclisis system comparable to
European Portuguese.

For zh-TW speakers, forms such as:

> chamo-me

and

> não me lembro

may initially appear to change word order unpredictably.

Use contrastive explanation carefully:

> Portuguese pronoun position is partly controlled by syntax.

Do not imply that placement is stylistic or interchangeable.

---

## 16. Word Order

Both languages commonly allow Subject–Verb–Object structures, which can provide
some positive transfer.

Chinese:

> 我喝咖啡。

Portuguese:

> Eu bebo café.

However, this similarity has limits.

Portuguese word order interacts with:

- pronouns,
- questions,
- adverbs,
- topicalization,
- discourse emphasis.

Chinese topic-comment structures should not be transferred mechanically into
Portuguese.

---

## 17. Topic-Prominent Chinese vs Portuguese

Traditional Chinese frequently uses topic-comment structures.

Example pattern:

> 這本書，我很喜歡。

Portuguese can topicalize elements, but this should not be assumed to function
identically.

At lower levels, prefer canonical Portuguese structures unless a marked
construction is being taught explicitly.

---

## 18. Questions

Chinese frequently forms questions using:

- question particles,
- interrogative words,
- A-not-A patterns,
- context.

Portuguese questions do not follow the same mechanisms.

Pay particular attention to:

- interrogative words,
- intonation,
- subject placement,
- clitic placement.

Do not infer Portuguese pronoun placement from Chinese question structure.

---

## 19. Negation

Chinese commonly uses forms such as:

- 不
- 沒
- 沒有

Portuguese commonly uses:

> não

but negation also interacts with clitic placement.

Example:

> Não me lembro.

For a Chinese-speaking learner, the important contrast is not only the negative
word itself but also the syntactic effect it has in Portuguese.

---

## 20. Possession

Chinese often uses:

> 的

to express possessive relationships.

Portuguese possessives behave differently and commonly include an article.

Example:

> o meu livro

> a minha mãe

Do not map `的` directly onto Portuguese possessive forms.

Teach the full Portuguese phrase.

---

## 21. Definite Articles with Personal Names

Traditional Chinese does not normally place definite articles before personal
names.

European Portuguese frequently does.

Examples:

> o Pedro

> a Ana

This may feel redundant to a Chinese-speaking learner.

Explain it as a natural PT-PT usage pattern rather than translating the article
literally.

---

## 22. Adjective Position

Chinese modifiers generally precede the noun.

Portuguese adjectives frequently follow the noun, although position varies with
meaning and usage.

Compare:

Chinese:

> 一間大房子

Portuguese:

> uma casa grande

This creates a common word-order transfer risk.

However, do not teach:

> Portuguese adjectives always follow nouns.

Some adjectives can precede nouns and the position may affect meaning or style.

---

## 23. Relative Modification

Chinese relative clauses normally precede the noun and use `的`.

Portuguese relative clauses generally follow the noun.

Compare conceptually:

Chinese:

> 我昨天買的書

Portuguese:

> o livro que comprei ontem

This difference is important as learners progress beyond simple sentences.

Do not transfer Chinese pre-nominal relative-clause order into Portuguese.

---

## 24. Classifiers

Traditional Chinese makes extensive use of classifiers such as:

- 個
- 本
- 杯
- 張
- 隻

Portuguese does not have a comparable general classifier system.

Do not search for a Portuguese equivalent of every Chinese classifier.

Portuguese usually expresses quantity through nouns, determiners, or measure
expressions.

---

## 25. Pronouns and Gender

Spoken Mandarin does not distinguish `他 / 她 / 它` phonologically.

Portuguese pronouns and agreement frequently make gender overt.

Examples:

> ele

> ela

A Chinese-speaking learner may need explicit attention to grammatical and
referential gender in Portuguese.

---

## 26. Politeness and Forms of Address

Chinese and European Portuguese both encode social relationships, but they do so
differently.

Traditional Chinese may use:

- 您,
- titles,
- kinship terms,
- role nouns,
- omission.

European Portuguese may use:

- tu,
- você,
- o senhor,
- a senhora,
- a person's name,
- titles,
- third-person constructions.

Do not map:

> 您 = você

as a universal equivalence.

Register must be explained contextually.

---

## 27. Zero Copula and Predicate Structures

Chinese permits certain adjectival or nominal predicate patterns that do not map
directly onto Portuguese.

For example, Chinese may form descriptions without an overt equivalent of an
English-style copula in structures where Portuguese requires a verb.

Learners should avoid transferring Chinese predicate structure directly.

Teach the Portuguese verb required by the target construction.

---

## 28. Pronunciation: Syllable Structure

Traditional Chinese and European Portuguese have significantly different sound
systems.

Chinese-speaking learners may find Portuguese consonant clusters and reduced
vowels difficult.

Potential areas include:

- complex syllable endings,
- consonant clusters,
- vowel reduction,
- nasal vowels,
- connected speech.

Do not assume that a learner can infer pronunciation accurately from Portuguese
orthography.

---

## 29. Unstressed Vowels

Traditional Chinese generally maintains clearer syllabic vowel realization than
European Portuguese.

PT-PT vowel reduction may therefore be difficult to perceive.

Written Portuguese may contain vowels that sound very weak in natural speech.

Chinese-speaking learners may initially pronounce every written vowel too
strongly.

Teach recognition before demanding perfect production.

---

## 30. Rhythm

Mandarin Chinese is often perceived as more syllable-timed than European
Portuguese, while PT-PT relies strongly on stress, reduction, and connected
speech.

A Chinese-speaking learner may produce Portuguese with overly even syllable
weight.

Focus on:

- stressed syllables,
- weak unstressed syllables,
- phrase rhythm.

---

## 31. Nasal Sounds

Portuguese nasal vowels and diphthongs do not correspond directly to Mandarin
nasal finals.

Do not explain Portuguese nasalization simply as adding `n` or `ng`.

Examples such as:

> pão

> não

> mãe

should be taught as Portuguese vowel patterns.

---

## 32. `r`, `lh`, and `nh`

Portuguese sounds represented by:

- `r`,
- `rr`,
- `lh`,
- `nh`

may not have straightforward Mandarin equivalents.

Avoid misleading transliterations using Chinese characters.

Prefer:

- audio,
- articulatory explanation,
- IPA when useful,
- approximate comparison only when clearly labeled.

---

## 33. Orthography

Traditional Chinese uses a morphosyllabic writing system.

Portuguese uses an alphabetic orthography.

Chinese-speaking learners may initially depend heavily on written Portuguese.

However, European Portuguese pronunciation often differs noticeably from a
letter-by-letter reading.

Teach spelling and pronunciation as related but distinct systems.

---

## 34. Positive Transfer Through English or Other Languages

Some zh-TW learners may already know English, German, Spanish, or another
European language.

A third language may help explain concepts such as:

- grammatical gender,
- articles,
- conjugation,
- agreement,
- tense,
- cognates.

Use such comparisons only when the learner actually benefits from them.

Do not require a third language for understanding.

---

## 35. Common zh-TW → pt-PT Transfer Risks

Common risks include:

- article omission,
- incorrect noun gender,
- incomplete agreement,
- unconjugated verbs,
- incorrect subject–verb agreement,
- excessive explicit subject pronouns,
- direct Chinese word order,
- missing prepositions,
- missing contractions,
- incorrect clitic placement,
- literal translations of `是`,
- literal translations of age expressions,
- adjective placement transfer,
- failure to mark tense where Portuguese requires it.

Use these as diagnostic possibilities, not assumptions about every learner.

---

## 36. Common pt-PT → zh-TW Transfer Risks

For Portuguese-speaking learners of Traditional Chinese, possible transfer risks
include:

- unnecessary copulas,
- unnecessary articles,
- unnecessary grammatical gender marking,
- tense over-marking,
- unnatural pronoun repetition,
- classifier omission,
- incorrect `的` usage,
- Portuguese-style relative-clause order,
- overuse of explicit subjects,
- literal transfer of Portuguese prepositions,
- unnatural politeness mapping.

These belong to the same language pair but should be emphasized only when the
learning direction is pt-PT → zh-TW.

---

## 37. Contrastive Explanation Format

When comparison is useful, prefer a compact structure:

> **pt-PT:**  
> Tenho vinte anos.
>
> **zh-TW:**  
> 我二十歲。
>
> **差異：**  
> 葡萄牙語使用 `ter`，中文不使用對應的「有」來表達年齡。

Avoid long comparative lectures unless the learner requests deeper analysis.

---

## 38. Translation Policy

Do not assume a one-to-one mapping between words or structures.

Prefer translating:

- meaning,
- grammatical function,
- register,
- natural usage.

When literal translation is pedagogically useful, label it clearly as literal.

Distinguish:

- literal translation,
- natural translation,
- structural explanation.

---

## 39. Bidirectional Use

This file is intentionally bidirectional.

For:

**zh-TW → pt-PT**

emphasize Portuguese structures that are absent or substantially different in
Chinese.

For:

**pt-PT → zh-TW**

emphasize Chinese structures that are absent or substantially different in
Portuguese.

Do not duplicate stable contrastive facts in separate directional files.

---

## 40. Core Principle

Use the differences between Traditional Chinese and European Portuguese to
predict useful teaching moments, not to stereotype learner errors.

Prefer:

**contrast → awareness → target-language production**

over:

**literal equivalence → memorization → transfer error**
