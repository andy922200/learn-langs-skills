# Traditional Chinese ↔ American English

Shared contrastive reference for:

- Traditional Chinese (`zh-TW`)
- American English (`en-US`)

This file describes structural differences, useful similarities, common transfer
risks, and comparison strategies between the two languages.

It is bidirectional.

Direction-specific teaching behavior belongs in `skills/`.

CEFR-level difficulty belongs in `shared/cefr/`.

General correction and retrieval behavior belongs in `shared/pedagogy/`.

American English language rules belong in `languages/en-US.md`.

Traditional Chinese language rules belong in `languages/zh-TW.md`.

---

## 1. Core Principle

Use contrastive explanation to help the learner understand differences between
Traditional Chinese and American English.

Do not force one language's grammar onto the other.

Prefer:

**identify difference → show contrast → produce target-language pattern**

over:

**literal translation → memorized equivalence**

---

## 2. Major Structural Contrast

Traditional Chinese and American English differ substantially in:

- articles and countability,
- plural marking,
- verb inflection and agreement,
- tense and aspect marking,
- the copula and adjectival predicates,
- subject omission,
- word order of time, place, and modifiers,
- questions and negation,
- relative clauses,
- clause connection and sentence boundaries,
- prepositions and phrasal verbs,
- politeness and register,
- pronunciation and rhythm.

These differences make direct word-for-word translation unreliable.

---

## 3. Useful Similarities

Some similarities can support positive transfer.

Both languages:

- normally use Subject–Verb–Object order in simple declarative sentences,
- place many modifiers before the noun,
- express many meanings with short function words and set phrases,
- have large sets of fixed collocations.

Example:

> 我喝咖啡。
> I drink coffee.

Make clear where the similarity ends. Both languages are SVO, but many other
word-order rules differ (see Sections 12–16).

---

## 4. Articles

Traditional Chinese does not have an article system comparable to English.

English uses `a / an`, `the`, and zero article. Article choice depends on
whether the noun is specific, already known, countable, or general.

Chinese may express definiteness through:

- demonstratives (這、那),
- word order,
- context.

Common transfer errors include:

- omitting articles,
- overusing `the`,
- using `a` with plural or uncountable nouns.

Do not explain articles merely as equivalents of `這` or `那`.

Teach articles as part of a noun phrase and through contrast:

> I need a pen. / The pen is on the table.

---

## 5. Countability and Plural Marking

Chinese nouns are generally not marked for plural in the same way as English
nouns.

Chinese often uses a number and a classifier:

> 兩本書

English marks plurality on the noun:

> two books

Common transfer errors include:

- *two book,
- *many information,
- *an advice,
- *informations, *equipments.

Traditional Chinese does not have a comparable obligatory countable/uncountable
distinction. Teach common uncountable nouns as collocations:

> a piece of advice, a lot of information

---

## 6. Classifiers vs Quantifiers

Traditional Chinese uses classifiers such as 個、本、杯、張、隻.

English does not have a general classifier system. It uses:

- plural nouns (`three books`),
- measure phrases (`a cup of tea`, `a piece of advice`),
- quantifiers (`some`, `many`, `much`, `a few`, `a little`).

Do not search for an English equivalent of every classifier.

`Much` and `many` depend on countability, which does not affect the Chinese
classifier choice in the same way.

---

## 7. Verb Inflection and Agreement

Traditional Chinese verbs do not change for person or number.

English present-tense verbs take `-s` with a third-person singular subject.

Compare:

> 他工作。
> He works.

Common transfer errors include:

- *He work.
- omitting `-s`,
- using `be` and another verb together, such as *She is work.

This error may persist even when the learner knows the rule. It is often a
production and attention problem, not only a knowledge problem.

---

## 8. Tense and Aspect

Traditional Chinese generally does not mark tense on the verb.

Chinese often relies on:

- context,
- time expressions,
- aspect markers such as 了, 過, 著, 在,
- particles.

English marks tense and aspect in the verb phrase.

Avoid one-to-one mappings such as:

> 了 = English past tense

The two systems are not equivalent.

Compare:

> 我吃了飯。→ I ate. / I have eaten.

> 我明天吃飯。→ I will eat tomorrow. / I'm eating tomorrow.

Common transfer risks:

- leaving out tense when a time expression is present,
- inconsistent tense within a paragraph,
- confusing the simple past and the present perfect,
- overusing the continuous form.

Chinese `過` does not map neatly to a single English tense either.

---

## 9. The Copula and Adjectival Predicates

Chinese adjectives can act as predicates without a copula.

> 他很高。

English requires `be`:

> He is tall.

Common transfer errors:

- *He tall.
- *He is very like it. (using `be` with a verb)

Chinese `是` is not equivalent to `be` in all uses.

Chinese `很` in a plain predicate adjective is often a structural marker, not a
strong "very".

> 他很高。 = He is tall. (not necessarily *very* tall)

Do not translate every `很` as `very`.

---

## 10. Subject Omission

Traditional Chinese frequently omits the subject when context makes it clear.

English normally requires an overt subject.

Compare:

> 下雨了。
> It's raining.

> 有一個問題。
> There is a problem.

Common transfer errors include:

- omitting the subject,
- omitting dummy `it` and `there`,
- using `have` instead of `there is` for existence.

Chinese `有` may correspond to `have` or `there is / there are`, depending on
the structure.

---

## 11. Topic-Comment Structure

Traditional Chinese frequently uses topic-comment structures.

> 這本書，我很喜歡。

English can topicalize an element, but does so less often and with special
prosody or emphasis.

Canonical:

> I like this book.

Do not transfer topic-comment structure mechanically into English.

At lower levels, prefer canonical English structures unless a marked
construction is being taught explicitly.

---

## 12. Word Order: Time and Place

Chinese usually places time and place expressions before the verb.

> 我每天在圖書館讀書。

English usually places place and then time after the verb phrase:

> I study at the library every day.

Common transfer error:

> *I every day at the library study.

Frequency adverbs may appear before the main verb in English:

> I usually study at the library.

Teach the common pattern through complete sentences, not through a rule alone.

---

## 13. Questions

Chinese questions may use:

- 嗎,
- 呢,
- A-not-A patterns (`你去不去？`),
- interrogative words in place,
- context and intonation.

English uses inversion, `do`-support, and wh-words at the beginning of the
clause.

> 你住在哪裡？
> Where do you live?

Common transfer errors include:

- *You live where?
- missing `do` support: *Where you live?
- *Do you can swim?

Embedded questions do not invert:

> I wonder where she lives.

---

## 14. Negation

Chinese commonly uses:

- 不
- 沒
- 沒有

English commonly uses `not` with an auxiliary, or `do`-support.

> 我不知道。
> I don't know.

> 我沒有錢。
> I don't have any money.

Common transfer errors include:

- *I no know.
- *I don't have no money.
- confusing `no` and `not`.

`沒有` corresponds to different English forms depending on structure
(`don't have`, `haven't`, `didn't`).

---

## 15. Relative Clauses and Modification

Chinese relative clauses precede the noun and usually use `的`.

> 我昨天買的書

English relative clauses follow the noun.

> the book that I bought yesterday

Common transfer errors include:

- *the I bought yesterday book,
- omitting the relative pronoun in cases where it is needed,
- using a resumptive pronoun: *the book that I bought it.

Short single-word adjectives usually precede the noun in both languages.

> 一間大房子
> a big house

---

## 16. Possession and `的`

Chinese `的` covers several relationships, including possession, modification,
and relative clauses.

English uses different structures:

- possessive determiners (`my`, `your`),
- `'s`,
- `of`,
- compounds.

> 我的書 = my book

> 老師的書 = the teacher's book

> 這個計畫的目標 = the goal of this plan

Do not map `的` onto a single English form.

Do not translate `的` as `of` in every case.

---

## 17. Prepositions and Localizers

Chinese often uses coverbs, localizers (上、裡、旁), and word order to express
relationships that English expresses with prepositions.

> 在桌子上 = on the table

> 在房間裡 = in the room

English prepositions (`in`, `on`, `at`, `for`, `to`, `of`) are not one-to-one with
Chinese words.

Common areas of difficulty include:

- time prepositions (`at`, `on`, `in`),
- verb + preposition collocations (`depend on`, `interested in`),
- omitting or adding prepositions (for example `discuss about`).

Teach prepositions in phrases and collocations, not as isolated equivalents.

---

## 18. Phrasal Verbs and Resultative Verbs

English uses phrasal verbs such as `turn on`, `give up`, `find out`.

Chinese has resultative verb compounds such as 打開、找到、看見.

There is partial conceptual overlap, but the systems are different.

Do not teach English phrasal verbs as a direct mapping to Chinese resultative
compounds.

Common difficulty:

- a phrasal verb has a non-literal meaning,
- particle choice must be memorized,
- a pronoun object goes between verb and particle (`turn it on`).

---

## 19. Clause Connection and Sentence Boundaries

Chinese often connects several clauses with commas within one sentence.

English requires a clear boundary or a connector.

Chinese:

> 這個計畫很好，太貴了。

English:

> The plan is good, but it is too expensive.

Common transfer error: a comma splice or run-on sentence.

> *The plan is good, it is too expensive.

---

## 20. Paired Connectors

Chinese often uses paired connectors:

- 雖然……但是……
- 因為……所以……

English normally uses only one connector in each pair.

> Although it was raining, we went out.

> Because it was raining, we stayed home.

Common transfer errors:

- *Although it was raining, but we went out.
- *Because it was raining, so we stayed home.

Teach connectors by function (concession, cause, result, contrast) and by how
they change the argument.

---

## 21. Modal Verbs

Chinese modal verbs (可以、能、會、要、應該) do not map one-to-one to English
modals.

For example, `會` may express:

- ability (`can`),
- future or prediction (`will`),
- likelihood (`would`).

`可以` may correspond to `can` or `may` depending on the function.

Do not map a Chinese modal to a single English modal.

Politeness in requests also differs. English often uses `could` / `would`:

> Could you help me?

---

## 22. Comparison

Chinese uses `比` in comparisons.

> 他比我高。

English uses a comparative form and `than`:

> He is taller than I am. / He is taller than me.

Common transfer errors:

- *He is more tall than me.
- *He is taller me.
- *He is more taller.

Short adjectives usually take `-er`. Longer adjectives usually take `more`.

---

## 23. Pronouns and Gender

Spoken Mandarin does not distinguish `他 / 她 / 它` phonologically.

English pronouns make gender and animacy overt: `he`, `she`, `it`.

A Chinese-speaking learner may confuse `he` and `she` in speech.

Singular `they` is standard in English for a person whose gender is unknown or
who uses `they`.

Chinese does not use reflexive pronouns in the same way as English. English
distinguishes `me` and `myself`, `him` and `himself`.

---

## 24. Politeness and Forms of Address

Both languages encode social relationships, but in different ways.

Traditional Chinese may use:

- 您,
- titles,
- kinship terms,
- role nouns.

English uses one second-person pronoun (`you`) and expresses politeness through:

- modal verbs,
- indirect requests,
- titles and names,
- tone.

Do not map:

> 您 = a specific English form

as a universal equivalence.

Register must be explained contextually.

---

## 25. Directness and Argument Structure

English argumentative writing usually expects a clear position and reasons,
often stated early.

Chinese writing may build context first and state the main point later, but this
depends on genre and writer.

Do not present this as a stereotype of all Chinese or English writing.

When a learner's English argument is hard to follow because the position is
late or implied, treat it as a task and audience choice:

> Can you state your position in the first sentence?

---

## 26. Collocations

Many English collocations do not correspond to Chinese by direct word
substitution.

Compare:

> 吃藥 → take medicine

> 看電視 → watch TV

> 開燈 → turn on the light

> 做決定 → make a decision

Do not assume that a Chinese verb maps to a single English verb.

Teach collocations as chunks and correct them through natural alternatives.

---

## 27. Pronunciation: Syllable Structure

Traditional Chinese and American English have different sound systems.

Chinese-speaking learners may find these areas difficult:

- final consonants and consonant clusters,
- unstressed vowel reduction,
- vowel length and tense/lax contrasts,
- connected speech.

A learner may add a vowel after a final consonant or omit a final consonant.

Do not assume that any individual learner will have all of these difficulties.

---

## 28. Pronunciation: Consonants

English sounds that may need attention for zh-TW speakers include:

- `/θ/` and `/ð/` (`think`, `this`),
- `/v/` and `/w/`,
- `/r/` and `/l/`,
- word-final stops and fricatives,
- `/ʃ/` and `/s/`.

Individual patterns vary. Use audio and minimal pairs rather than assuming a
fixed error list.

Avoid transliterating English sounds with Chinese characters.

---

## 29. Rhythm, Stress, and Intonation

Mandarin is a tonal language. English is not, but English has word stress and
sentence stress.

A Chinese-speaking learner may:

- give every syllable equal weight,
- not reduce unstressed vowels,
- not stress key content words,
- transfer Chinese lexical tone habits into English intonation.

Teach stress and rhythm through phrases and sentences, not only single words.

---

## 30. Orthography

Traditional Chinese uses a morphosyllabic writing system.

English uses an alphabetic orthography with an irregular relationship between
spelling and sound.

Chinese-speaking learners may initially rely heavily on written English.

Teach spelling and pronunciation as related but distinct systems.

Do not infer pronunciation reliably from spelling.

---

## 31. Positive Transfer

Some transfer can help.

Possible bridges include:

- similar basic SVO word order,
- pre-nominal adjectives,
- English loanwords already used in Taiwanese Mandarin,
- academic vocabulary the learner already knows in Chinese.

Loanwords and Chinese phrases inspired by English should be checked, because
meaning may not match exactly.

Do not require the learner to know another language for understanding.

---

## 32. Common zh-TW → en-US Transfer Risks

Common risks include:

- article omission or overuse,
- incomplete plural marking,
- countability errors,
- missing third-person `-s`,
- missing `be` or an extra `be`,
- inconsistent tense,
- missing subjects,
- word order of time and place,
- missing `do`-support in questions and negation,
- Chinese relative-clause order,
- comma splices,
- paired connectors,
- literal translation of `的`,
- literal collocations,
- confusing `he` and `she`,
- overly direct or overly indirect register.

Use these as diagnostic possibilities, not assumptions about every learner.

---

## 33. Common en-US → zh-TW Transfer Risks

For English-speaking learners of Traditional Chinese, possible transfer risks
include:

- classifier omission or incorrect classifier choice,
- overuse of `了` as a past-tense marker,
- incorrect `的` usage,
- English-style relative-clause order,
- unnecessary subject pronouns,
- using `是` where a predicate adjective is needed,
- missing or incorrect `很`,
- tone errors,
- word order of time and place,
- literal transfer of English prepositions and collocations,
- unnatural politeness mapping.

These belong to the same language pair but should be emphasized only when the
learning direction is en-US → zh-TW.

---

## 34. Contrastive Explanation Format

When comparison is useful, prefer a compact structure:

> **en-US:**  
> He is tall.
>
> **zh-TW:**  
> 他很高。
>
> **差異：**  
> 英文需要動詞 `be`；中文的形容詞可以直接作謂語，`很` 不一定表示「非常」。

Avoid long comparative lectures unless the learner requests deeper analysis.

---

## 35. Translation Policy

Do not assume a one-to-one mapping between words or structures.

Prefer translating:

- meaning,
- grammatical function,
- register,
- natural usage.

When literal translation is pedagogically useful, label it clearly as literal.

Distinguish:

- literal translation,
- natural translation,
- structural explanation.

---

## 36. Bidirectional Use

This file is intentionally bidirectional.

For:

**zh-TW → en-US**

emphasize English structures that are absent or substantially different in
Chinese.

For:

**en-US → zh-TW**

emphasize Chinese structures that are absent or substantially different in
English.

Do not duplicate stable contrastive facts in separate directional files.

---

## 37. Core Principle

Use the differences between Traditional Chinese and American English to predict
useful teaching moments, not to stereotype learner errors.

Prefer:

**contrast → awareness → target-language production**

over:

**literal equivalence → memorization → transfer error**
