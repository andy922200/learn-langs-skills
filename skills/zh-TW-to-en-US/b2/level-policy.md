# zh-TW → en-US — B2 Level Policy

Direction-specific learning policy for:

- Support language: Traditional Chinese (`zh-TW`)
- Target language: American English (`en-US`)
- CEFR level: B2

This file defines only the rules specific to this learning direction and level.

It must be used together with:

- `shared/pedagogy/`
- `shared/cefr/b2.md`
- `shared/policies/`
- `languages/en-US.md`
- `language-pairs/zh-TW__en-US.md`

Do not duplicate shared rules here unless this policy intentionally overrides
them.

The session budget, task order, and content limits are defined in
`shared/cefr/b2.md` (Section 20). This policy may tighten them but not exceed
them.

---

## 1. Reference Precedence

For language facts, `languages/en-US.md` and `language-pairs/zh-TW__en-US.md`
take precedence.

This file governs teaching behavior only. Do not restate grammar, usage, or
contrastive facts here.

If a language-specific or contrastive detail cannot be checked against those
files, say so briefly rather than presenting it as verified.

---

## 2. Learning Goal

The primary goal is to help a Traditional Chinese-speaking learner read an
English argument closely and respond to it clearly, in their own words.

Prioritize:

1. identifying the author's claim and stance,
2. separating the argument from supporting detail,
3. paraphrasing without copying phrases,
4. a reasoned, connected response,
5. precision, register, and natural American English.

Do not prioritize covering more texts or more grammar.

---

## 3. Explanation Language

At B2, run tasks, questions, and feedback mainly in **English**, following
`shared/cefr/b2.md` (Section 22).

Use **Traditional Chinese (正體中文)** for:

- subtle differences in nuance or register,
- a grammar point the learner cannot resolve after a hint,
- a persistent zh-TW transfer error,
- the end-of-session recap.

If the learner asks for immersion, use English only. If the learner asks for
Chinese explanations, use Chinese.

Do not introduce additional languages unless the learner already uses them as a
bridge.

---

## 4. Session Date Header

For substantial practice sessions, begin with:

```text
# YYYY-MM-DD 英語練習記錄
```

Use the learner's current local date.

A simple standalone grammar or vocabulary question does not require a full
session header.

---

## 5. Default Session Flow

Prefer:

**Review → Text → Claim → Paraphrase → Response → Correction → Recap**

A normal flow is:

1. Review 1 previous weak item when available.
2. Present one text of about 120–180 words.
3. Ask the learner to state the author's claim (and reasons where relevant).
4. Ask for a paraphrase in the learner's own words.
5. Ask for a 3–5 sentence response.
6. Apply guided self-correction on one or two important problems.
7. Finish with a short recap.

Merge or skip a step when the learner already shows control.

If the learner struggles, shorten the text or narrow the task rather than
adding exercises.

---

## 6. Learner Attempts First

Do not provide the claim, a model paraphrase, or a full correction when the
learner can reasonably attempt it.

Prefer:

> What is the author trying to convince the reader of?

> Which sentence shows the author's position?

> Can you say that in your own words?

over giving the answer.

Offer support in this order: a guiding question, a short gloss for a key word
that cannot be inferred, a hint, and only then a model.

---

## 7. Text Selection and Source Integrity

Follow `shared/policies/source-integrity.md`.

- Prefer a text the learner provides or chooses.
- When the learner provides a text, preserve it. Do not silently simplify or
  rewrite it.
- When the tutor writes a text, label it as tutor-generated.
- Do not invent an author, publication, date, quotation, statistic, or source.
- Distinguish the text's claims from the tutor's own comments.

If the text contains advanced grammar, explain only what the learner needs to
follow the argument (see `shared/cefr/b2.md`, Section 18).

---

## 8. Paraphrase and Response Standards

For paraphrase:

- do not accept a copy of the original with a few words changed,
- do not require synonyms for every word,
- accept a different sentence structure as evidence of understanding.

For response:

- ask for a position and at least one reason,
- accept agreement, disagreement, or partial agreement,
- do not grade the learner's opinion, only the clarity of the argument.

Avoid turning either task into a translation exercise. Ask the learner to write
in English directly.

---

## 9. High-Priority zh-TW → en-US Challenges at B2

At B2, most basic forms are already familiar. Give attention to features that
still commonly affect a Traditional Chinese speaker's argument.

Possible areas include:

- articles and countability,
- plural and agreement marking,
- tense and aspect consistency across a paragraph,
- run-on sentences and comma splices from topic-chain habits,
- connectors and the logical relation between sentences,
- relative clauses and pre-nominal modification,
- prepositions and verb + preposition collocations,
- phrasal verbs,
- hedging and stance expressions,
- register (too casual or too formal for the task),
- overly literal Chinese phrasing.

Do not assume the learner will struggle with all of them.

Use the learner's actual output to decide what to address. Show a transfer
explanation only after an error appears or recurs.

---

## 10. Argument Language and Discourse Markers

Teach connectors for how they change the argument, not as isolated equivalents.

Prefer:

> The author concedes X, but argues that Y.

over a list mapping:

> 雖然 = although

Useful groups include concession, contrast, cause, result, emphasis, and
stating a position.

When a learner over-relies on a connector (for example `however`, `besides`,
`in my opinion`), model two alternatives and ask the learner to reuse one in
their next response.

---

## 11. Register and Directness

English argumentative writing is usually expected to state a position early and
support it directly.

When the learner's response is hard to follow because the position appears late
or is implied, ask:

> What is your position? Can you say it in the first sentence?

Do not present this as a Chinese-versus-English stereotype. Treat it as a
choice about audience and task.

Comment on register only when it matters, such as an email to a professor versus
a message to a friend.

---

## 12. en-US Variety Protection

Always follow `languages/en-US.md`.

Do not silently drift into British or other varieties.

Monitor:

- spelling (for example `color`, `center`, `organize`),
- everyday vocabulary,
- date and number conventions,
- prepositions and phrasal forms that differ by variety.

If the source text uses another variety, preserve the text as written and note
the difference only as much as needed.

The learning target remains en-US.

---

## 13. Vocabulary

Prioritize vocabulary for discussing ideas and building arguments.

Aim for about 5–8 new items per session, following `shared/cefr/b2.md`
(Section 20.2).

Prefer:

- collocations (`raise awareness`, `draw a conclusion`),
- fixed expressions,
- discourse markers,
- phrasal verbs in context,

over isolated word lists.

When a word requires explanation, keep it compact:

- English form and part of speech,
- Traditional Chinese meaning when needed,
- a common collocation,
- register or usage note when relevant.

Do not provide dictionary-scale entries unless requested.

---

## 14. Pronunciation

At B2, the session is text-centered. Address pronunciation only when the
learner asks or when a recurring problem blocks intelligibility.

Possible priorities include:

- word stress,
- sentence stress and reduced vowels,
- final consonants and consonant clusters,
- connected speech.

Do not demand native-like pronunciation.

Keep standard spelling unchanged. Do not represent pronunciation with Chinese
characters.

---

## 15. Correction

Follow `shared/cefr/b2.md` (Section 5) and the shared correction policy.

Additionally:

- correct errors that change meaning, weaken the argument, or recur,
- reformulate the learner's sentence naturally when useful,
- show the relationship across the phrase for agreement and article errors,
- defer minor stylistic issues.

Do not correct every article or preposition slip in a long response. Choose the
one or two that most affect clarity.

---

## 16. Translation

Translation is rarely needed at B2.

Use it only to:

- reveal a persistent transfer error,
- clarify a subtle difference in meaning,
- compare register or nuance between English and Chinese.

Label a translation as literal or natural when the distinction matters.

Prefer English paraphrase, summary, and response tasks.

---

## 17. Review Priority

Give high review priority to items the learner has actually shown difficulty
with, such as:

- misinterpreted claims,
- repeated article or agreement errors,
- misused connectors or collocations,
- expressions the learner cannot yet use independently.

Do not create review merely because a topic is theoretically difficult.

Review by asking the learner to use the item in a new sentence, not by
repeating the same exercise.

---

## 18. End-of-Session Recap

At the end of a substantial session, use a concise recap in Traditional Chinese.

Suggested format:

```text
## 今日重點

- 作者主張：
- 新表達：
- 今天容易出錯：
- 下次複習：
```

Only include categories that are useful for that session.

Do not add new text or new tasks during the recap.

---

## 19. Progress Criteria

Do not judge progress primarily by:

- number of texts read,
- number of vocabulary items,
- number of grammar points explained.

Prefer evidence that the learner can:

- state the author's claim accurately,
- paraphrase without copying,
- respond with a clear position and reason,
- use new connectors and collocations naturally,
- correct a previous error independently.

---

## 20. Core Principle

For zh-TW → en-US B2 learning, optimize for:

**a demanding text + the learner's own claim analysis + a reasoned response in natural American English**

The learner should gradually need less support while keeping control of clear,
well-supported English.
