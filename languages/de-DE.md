# German as Used in Germany (de-DE)

Shared language reference for **German as used in Germany (de-DE)**.

This file defines stable grammar, usage, pronunciation, and regional conventions
for Standard German as used in Germany.

It is level-independent and may be reused across CEFR levels and learning
directions.

General teaching behavior belongs in `shared/pedagogy/`.

CEFR difficulty and scaffolding belong in `shared/cefr/`.

Cross-language comparison belongs in `language-pairs/`.

---

## 1. Default Variety

Always treat **Standard German as used in Germany (de-DE)** as the target
variety.

Unless another variety is explicitly requested, generated language should follow
natural usage in Germany.

Prefer:

- de-DE spelling (official orthography, including `ß`),
- de-DE vocabulary,
- de-DE punctuation and formatting conventions,
- de-DE date, number, and unit conventions,
- de-DE pronunciation guidance (Standard German, *Standardaussprache*).

Do not silently drift toward Austrian (de-AT) or Swiss (de-CH) German, or toward
a regional dialect.

Standard German is a written and spoken standard. Everyday speech in Germany
varies by region. Treat regional speech as variation, not as error (see
Section 24).

---

## 2. Verb Position

German verb position is the core of its sentence structure.

In a main clause, the finite verb is in **second position**:

> Ich lerne heute Deutsch.

> Heute lerne ich Deutsch.

The subject does not have to come first. When another element comes first, the
subject follows the verb (inversion).

In a subordinate clause, the finite verb goes to the **end**:

> Ich weiß, dass er heute kommt.

> Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Berlin arbeiten möchte.

Not natural:

> *Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich möchte in Berlin arbeiten.

Yes/no questions and imperatives place the verb **first**:

> Kommst du morgen?

> Komm bitte pünktlich.

---

## 3. Verbal Bracket

In many verb phrases, German splits the verb into two parts. The finite verb is
in second position and the other part goes to the end of the clause.

Modal verbs and the infinitive:

> Ich möchte morgen früh aufstehen.

Perfect tense and the past participle:

> Ich habe gestern einen Film gesehen.

Separable verbs and the prefix:

> Der Zug fährt um acht Uhr ab.

The words between the two parts are the **middle field**. Time, manner, and place
information normally go there.

Do not present the bracket as an exception. It is the normal structure and
should be taught early through examples.

---

## 4. Noun Gender

German nouns have three grammatical genders: masculine (`der`), feminine
(`die`), and neuter (`das`).

> der Tisch, die Lampe, das Buch

Gender is often not predictable from meaning:

> das Mädchen (neuter), die Sonne, der Mond

Some endings are reliable indicators, with exceptions:

> -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft → feminine
> -chen, -lein → neuter
> -er (persons) → often masculine

Teach a noun together with its article and plural form:

> der Tisch, die Tische

Do not present a gender rule as exceptionless. Do not explain gender by
appealing to the meaning of the word.

---

## 5. Plural Formation

German plurals have several patterns:

> das Buch → die Bücher
> der Tag → die Tage
> die Frau → die Frauen
> das Auto → die Autos
> der Lehrer → die Lehrer

Plural patterns are only partly predictable. Teach the plural with the noun as
part of the vocabulary item.

The definite article in the plural is `die` for all genders.

---

## 6. Cases

German has four cases: nominative, accusative, dative, and genitive.

Cases are marked mainly on articles, pronouns, and adjectives, and only
partially on nouns.

Basic functions:

- nominative: subject,
- accusative: direct object,
- dative: indirect object,
- genitive: possession and some fixed uses.

> Der Mann sieht den Hund. (nominative, accusative)

> Ich gebe dem Kind das Buch. (dative, accusative)

Only masculine changes between nominative and accusative in the definite
article:

> der Mann → den Mann

In the feminine, neuter, and plural, the nominative and accusative are the same.

Some nouns add a case ending (`dem Kind`, `den Kindern`). The weak masculine
nouns take `-(e)n` in all cases except the nominative singular:

> der Student → den Studenten

Teach cases through the meaning and function in a sentence, not through tables
alone.

---

## 7. Definite and Indefinite Articles

The definite article changes with gender, number, and case:

| | masc. | fem. | neut. | pl. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | der | die | das | die |
| accusative | den | die | das | die |
| dative | dem | der | dem | den |
| genitive | des | der | des | der |

The indefinite article `ein` and the negative `kein` follow a similar pattern:

> ein Mann, eine Frau, ein Kind
> kein Mann, keine Frau, kein Kind

There is no indefinite article in the plural, but there is `keine`.

Article use resembles English in many places, with some differences:

> Ich bin Lehrer. (no article for a profession)

> Ich gehe in die Schule. / Ich bin in der Schule.

Teach the table as a reference and the forms through high-frequency phrases.

---

## 8. Adjective Endings

An adjective before a noun takes an ending. The ending depends on gender,
number, case, and on the article before the adjective.

> der große Mann
> ein großer Mann
> großer Mann (no article)

After a definite article, the endings are mostly `-e` or `-en`. After no article
or an indefinite article, the ending often carries the case information that the
article does not show.

An adjective used after a verb such as `sein` has no ending:

> Der Mann ist groß.

Teach adjective endings gradually, and only in the patterns the learner needs.
Do not present the full paradigm to a new learner.

---

## 9. Pronouns

Personal pronouns change with case:

| nominative | accusative | dative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ich | mich | mir |
| du | dich | dir |
| er | ihn | ihm |
| sie | sie | ihr |
| es | es | ihm |
| wir | uns | uns |
| ihr | euch | euch |
| sie / Sie | sie / Sie | ihnen / Ihnen |

Pronouns refer to the **grammatical gender** of the noun, not to natural gender:

> Der Tisch ist neu. Er ist groß.

> Das Mädchen ist neu hier. Es ist freundlich. (or `sie` for a person, by
> meaning)

Reflexive pronouns (`sich`) appear with many verbs and should be taught as part
of the verb:

> sich freuen, sich erinnern, sich waschen

---

## 10. Present Tense and Verb Conjugation

Regular verbs take endings by person:

> ich lerne, du lernst, er lernt, wir lernen, ihr lernt, sie lernen

Some verbs change their stem vowel in the `du` and `er/sie/es` forms:

> fahren → du fährst, er fährt
> sprechen → du sprichst, er spricht
> lesen → du liest, er liest

`sein` and `haben` are irregular:

> ich bin, du bist, er ist; ich habe, du hast, er hat

The present tense also expresses the future when a time expression is present:

> Morgen fahre ich nach München.

Do not omit the ending because the meaning is clear.

---

## 11. Perfect and Simple Past

German has two main past forms.

The **perfect** (*Perfekt*) uses `haben` or `sein` plus the past participle. It
is normal in everyday speech and informal writing:

> Ich habe Deutsch gelernt.

> Er ist nach Berlin gefahren.

The **simple past** (*Präteritum*) is normal in written narrative, news, and
with many common verbs in speech:

> Ich war müde.

> Sie hatte keine Zeit.

> Es gab viele Leute.

Prefer the Präteritum for `sein`, `haben`, and modal verbs in speech, and the
Perfekt for most other verbs.

Choose `sein` as the auxiliary for verbs of movement or change of state and for
`sein`, `bleiben`, and `werden`:

> Ich bin gegangen. Er ist geblieben.

Otherwise use `haben`.

Do not present Perfekt and Präteritum as differing in time reference.

---

## 12. Past Participles

Regular (weak) verbs: `ge-` + stem + `-t`:

> lernen → gelernt

Irregular (strong) verbs usually take `ge-` + stem + `-en` and often change the
vowel:

> sehen → gesehen, gehen → gegangen

Separable verbs put `ge-` between prefix and stem:

> aufstehen → aufgestanden

Inseparable prefixes and verbs ending in `-ieren` have no `ge-`:

> besuchen → besucht, studieren → studiert

Teach the participle of common verbs as part of the verb.

---

## 13. Future

German expresses the future mainly with the **present tense** and a time
expression:

> Nächste Woche fliege ich nach Wien.

`werden` + infinitive marks a prediction, assumption, or promise:

> Es wird morgen regnen.

> Er wird wohl zu Hause sein.

Do not present `werden` as the general equivalent of an English future.

---

## 14. Modal Verbs

The modals are `können`, `müssen`, `dürfen`, `sollen`, `wollen`, `mögen`
(`möchte`).

The modal is in second position and the infinitive goes to the end:

> Ich muss heute arbeiten.

Modals are irregular in the singular:

> ich kann, du kannst, er kann

Common meanings:

- `müssen`: necessity,
- `können`: ability or possibility,
- `dürfen`: permission,
- `sollen`: a demand or recommendation from someone else,
- `wollen`: intention,
- `möchte`: a polite wish.

`nicht müssen` means lack of necessity, not prohibition:

> Du musst nicht kommen. (You do not have to come.)

> Du darfst nicht kommen. (You are not allowed to come.)

Teach the difference explicitly, as it is often confused.

---

## 15. Separable and Inseparable Verbs

Many German verbs have a prefix. Some prefixes separate in a main clause, and
some never do.

Separable (stressed prefix):

> anrufen → Ich rufe dich an.

> aufstehen → Er steht früh auf.

Inseparable (unstressed prefix):

> verstehen → Ich verstehe dich.

> bekommen → Sie bekommt ein Geschenk.

Some prefixes can be either. Learn them as vocabulary items.

In a subordinate clause and in the infinitive, the separable verb stays together:

> Ich weiß, dass er früh aufsteht.

> Ich möchte dich anrufen.

---

## 16. Word Order in Main Clauses

The finite verb is in position two. Other elements are ordered mainly by
information structure.

A neutral order in the middle field is often:

> time – manner – place

> Ich fahre morgen mit dem Zug nach Hamburg.

A pronoun object normally comes before a noun object:

> Ich gebe es dem Kind. / Ich gebe ihm das Buch.

`nicht` position depends on what it negates. When it negates the whole
sentence, it comes near the end, before the second part of the bracket:

> Ich habe ihn nicht gesehen.

> Ich gehe heute nicht ins Kino.

Do not present word order as free. It is flexible within rules.

---

## 17. Questions and Negation

Yes/no questions put the verb first:

> Sprichst du Deutsch?

Wh-questions put a question word first and the verb second:

> Wo wohnst du?

> Wann kommt er?

Negation uses `nicht` and `kein`.

`kein` negates a noun that would take an indefinite article or no article:

> Ich habe keinen Hund.

> Ich habe keine Zeit.

`nicht` negates a verb, an adjective, an adverb, or a noun with a definite
article:

> Ich kenne den Mann nicht.

> Das ist nicht gut.

German does not use `nicht` and `kein` together to negate the same element:

> Ich habe kein Geld. (not *Ich habe nicht kein Geld.)

---

## 18. Subordinate Clauses and Connectors

German connectors fall into groups by their effect on word order.

Coordinating conjunctions do not change word order (verb in position two):

> und, aber, oder, denn, sondern

> Ich bleibe zu Hause, denn ich bin krank.

Subordinating conjunctions send the finite verb to the end:

> dass, weil, wenn, ob, obwohl, als, während, damit

> Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich krank bin.

Adverbs used as connectors (`deshalb`, `deswegen`, `trotzdem`, `dann`) occupy
the first position and are followed by the verb:

> Ich bin krank, deshalb bleibe ich zu Hause.

Commas separate main and subordinate clauses in German, and the comma before
`dass` and `weil` is required.

`wenn` (conditions and repeated events), `als` (one event in the past), and
`ob` (indirect yes/no questions) should be taught by contrast and in context.

---

## 19. Relative Clauses

Relative clauses follow the noun and are separated by commas.

The relative pronoun agrees with the noun in gender and number and takes its
case from its function in the relative clause:

> Der Mann, der dort steht, ist mein Lehrer.

> Der Mann, den ich gestern gesehen habe, ist mein Lehrer.

The finite verb goes to the end of the relative clause.

The relative pronoun cannot be omitted, unlike in English.

---

## 20. Prepositions

German prepositions govern a case.

Accusative: `durch`, `für`, `gegen`, `ohne`, `um`.

Dative: `aus`, `bei`, `mit`, `nach`, `seit`, `von`, `zu`.

Genitive (formal, and often dative in speech): `wegen`, `trotz`, `während`.

Two-way prepositions take the accusative for **movement toward** a place and the
dative for **location**:

> `an`, `auf`, `hinter`, `in`, `neben`, `über`, `unter`, `vor`, `zwischen`

> Ich gehe in die Schule. (accusative)

> Ich bin in der Schule. (dative)

Prepositions are also fixed with particular verbs and cannot be predicted from
meaning:

> warten auf (+ acc.), sich freuen über (+ acc.), Angst haben vor (+ dat.)

Some prepositions contract with the article:

> in dem → im, zu dem → zum, zu der → zur, an dem → am

Teach verb + preposition combinations as chunks. Do not present a preposition as
having one universal equivalent in another language.

---

## 21. Compounds and Word Formation

German forms long compounds by joining words:

> die Handschuhe (hand + shoe = glove)

The **last** element determines the gender and the basic meaning:

> der Tisch + die Lampe → die Tischlampe

Compounds are written as one word. Do not split them in the English manner.

Some compounds use a linking element:

> die Geburt + der Tag → der Geburtstag

Derivation with prefixes and suffixes is productive:

> -ung (noun from verb), -lich, -bar, un-

Teach how to break down a compound as a reading and vocabulary strategy.

---

## 22. Sie, du, and Forms of Address

German has an informal `du` and a formal `Sie`.

Use `Sie` (always capitalized) for adults in formal and service situations, and
for strangers by default. Use `du` for friends, family, children, and among
many colleagues and young people.

The verb form for `Sie` is the same as for the third person plural:

> Wie heißen Sie? / Wie heißt du?

Moving from `Sie` to `du` is often offered by the older or higher-status person.

Titles and surnames are common in formal settings:

> Herr Müller, Frau Dr. Schmidt

Do not map `Sie` to Chinese `您` as a universal equivalent. Register must be
explained by context.

---

## 23. Spelling and Orthography

German follows the official orthography.

Nouns are capitalized:

> der Tisch, die Freiheit, Ich lerne Deutsch.

The letters `ä`, `ö`, `ü`, and `ß` are part of the alphabet. If they cannot be
typed, use `ae`, `oe`, `ue`, `ss`, but do not do this by default.

`ß` is used after long vowels and diphthongs (`Straße`, `heiß`). `ss` is used
after short vowels (`dass`, `muss`). In Germany, `ß` is used. Do not follow the
Swiss convention of replacing `ß` with `ss`.

Numbers, dates, and quotation marks follow German conventions (see Section 25).

Use one spelling system consistently within a text.

---

## 24. Regional and National Variety

German is a pluricentric language. The main standard varieties are used in
Germany (de-DE), Austria (de-AT), and Switzerland (de-CH).

Some common differences:

| de-DE | Common de-AT | Common de-CH |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Januar | Jänner | Januar |
| Brötchen | Semmel | Brötli |
| Tüte | Sackerl | Sack |
| Kartoffeln | Erdäpfel | Kartoffeln |
| Sahne | Schlagobers | Rahm |
| Fahrrad | Fahrrad | Velo |

The perfect of `sitzen`, `stehen`, and `liegen` uses `haben` in northern Germany
and `sein` in the south, Austria, and Switzerland. Both may appear.

Within Germany, there are regional differences in vocabulary:

> Brötchen / Schrippe / Semmel, Samstag / Sonnabend

Prefer the forms most widely understood in Germany. Explain a variety difference
only as much as needed.

If a learner-provided text uses another variety or a regional form, preserve it
(see Section 29).

---

## 25. Punctuation, Dates, Numbers, and Units

German conventions include:

- comma between main and subordinate clauses and before relative clauses,
- comma as decimal separator (`3,5`),
- period or a space as thousands separator (`1.000`),
- date order `day.month.year` (for example `30.9.2026`),
- 24-hour time (`18:30 Uhr`),
- metric units (kilometers, kilograms, Celsius),
- currency: the euro, written `5,50 €`.

German quotation marks are `„…“`. Use them when quoting in German.

State the date format when ambiguity could cause confusion.

---

## 26. Pronunciation Variety

Use **Standard German (*Hochlautung*)** as the default pronunciation reference.

Standard German is a convenient teaching reference, not the only accent. Do not
present it as the only correct pronunciation.

Key features include:

- spelling is largely phonemic and more predictable than in English,
- long and short vowels are distinct and often marked by the following
  consonants,
- final voiced consonants are devoiced (`Hund` /hʊnt/, `Tag` /taːk/),
- word-initial stress on the first syllable in many native words.

Spelling is a reasonably reliable guide to pronunciation once a small number of
patterns are learned.

---

## 27. Sounds Often Needing Attention

Sounds that are often difficult for learners from many first languages include:

- `ü` and `ö` (front rounded vowels),
- the `ch` sounds: `ich` [ç] and `ach` [x],
- `r` (uvular or vocalized),
- `z` [ts], `pf` [pf], `sp` and `st` at the start of a word ([ʃp], [ʃt]),
- vowel length contrast (`Stadt` / `Staat`),
- aspiration of `p`, `t`, `k`,
- the glottal stop before a word beginning with a vowel,
- consonant clusters (`Strumpf`, `Herbst`).

Use audio, articulation description, and minimal pairs. Use IPA only when it
helps.

Avoid transliterating German sounds with characters from another writing system.

---

## 28. Stress, Rhythm, and Reduction

German is often described as stress-timed.

Stress often falls on the first syllable of a native word:

> ARbeit, LEhrer

Words with an inseparable prefix are stressed on the stem, and words with a
separable prefix on the prefix:

> beSUchen (inseparable), ANrufen (separable)

Unstressed `-e`, `-en`, `-er` are reduced:

> lernen, Lehrer

Sentence stress usually falls on the most informative word.

Teach recognition of reduction before requiring production of it.

## 29. Source Fidelity

When learning material is supplied by the learner:

- preserve the source text exactly,
- do not silently correct spelling, dialect, or national variety,
- distinguish the source from tutor-generated content.

If the source uses Austrian or Swiss vocabulary or spelling, or an older
orthography, preserve it and note the difference only as much as needed.

Follow `shared/policies/source-integrity.md`.

---

## 30. Generated Examples

Generated examples should:

- be natural Standard German as used in Germany,
- match the learner's level,
- use current, ordinary usage,
- avoid invented facts, quotations, or sources presented as real.

Do not present tutor-generated text as if it came from a real publication.

Use a small number of clear examples rather than many similar ones.

Give the article and, where useful, the plural with a new noun.

---

## 31. Avoid False Absolutes

Do not state rules more strongly than German usage supports.

Avoid:

> German word order is free.

> The verb is always at the end.

> Präteritum is only used in writing.

Prefer:

> Usually…

> In a subordinate clause, …

> In everyday speech, many speakers prefer…

When usage varies by register, region, or variety, say so briefly.

---

## 32. Naturalness vs Correctness

A sentence may be grammatical and still sound unnatural.

Distinguish:

- ungrammatical,
- grammatical but unnatural,
- natural but informal or regional,
- natural in another variety.

When giving feedback, say which kind of issue it is. Do not label every
unnatural sentence as wrong.

Where useful, offer a more natural alternative and explain the difference in one
sentence.

---

## 33. Core Principle

Treat German as a living language used across many registers and regions.

Prefer:

**natural de-DE + clear explanation + honest note about variation**

over:

**rigid rule-giving + false absolutes + silent variety drift**
