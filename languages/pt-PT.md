# European Portuguese (pt-PT)

Shared language reference for **European Portuguese (Português Europeu / PT-PT)**.

This file defines stable grammar, usage, pronunciation, and regional conventions
for European Portuguese.

It is level-independent and may be reused across CEFR levels and learning
directions.

General teaching behavior belongs in `shared/pedagogy/`.

CEFR difficulty and scaffolding belong in `shared/cefr/`.

Cross-language comparison belongs in `language-pairs/`.

---

## 1. Default Variety

Always treat **European Portuguese (PT-PT)** as the target variety.

Unless another variety is explicitly requested, generated language should follow
natural usage in Portugal.

Prefer:

- PT-PT grammar,
- PT-PT vocabulary,
- PT-PT pronoun usage,
- PT-PT clitic placement,
- PT-PT forms of address,
- PT-PT pronunciation guidance.

Do not silently drift toward Brazilian Portuguese.

---

## 2. Subject Pronouns

Portuguese is a pro-drop language.

Subject pronouns may be omitted when the verb form or context makes the subject
clear.

Natural:

> Moro em Lisboa.

Also possible:

> Eu moro em Lisboa.

Use explicit subject pronouns when they are useful for:

- contrast,
- emphasis,
- clarity,
- learner scaffolding.

Do not force subject pronouns into every generated sentence.

---

## 3. Grammatical Gender

Portuguese nouns have grammatical gender.

When introducing nouns, include the article whenever useful.

Prefer:

> a casa

rather than only:

> casa

Prefer:

> o livro

rather than only:

> livro

Common endings may provide useful tendencies, but they must not be presented as
exceptionless rules.

---

## 4. Number and Agreement

Articles, nouns, adjectives, demonstratives, and other agreeing elements should
match appropriately in gender and number.

Examples:

> o livro novo

> os livros novos

> a casa branca

> as casas brancas

When explaining agreement, treat the words as a grammatical relationship rather
than isolated forms.

---

## 5. Definite Articles Before Personal Names

European Portuguese commonly uses definite articles before personal names.

Examples:

> o Nuno

> a Isabel

> O Pedro mora em Lisboa.

> A Ana trabalha aqui.

This is common and natural in Portugal.

Do not present it as an absolute rule for every register, dialect, or context.

---

## 6. Possessives

Possessive determiners commonly occur with definite articles.

Examples:

> o meu livro

> a minha mãe

> os meus amigos

> as minhas coisas

For learners, it is often useful to teach the complete phrase as a reusable
pattern.

---

## 7. Preposition + Article Contractions

Portuguese regularly contracts certain prepositions with articles.

### `de`

- de + o → do
- de + a → da
- de + os → dos
- de + as → das

### `em`

- em + o → no
- em + a → na
- em + os → nos
- em + as → nas

### `a`

- a + o → ao
- a + a → à
- a + os → aos
- a + as → às

Examples:

> Sou do Porto.

> Moro na Alemanha.

> Vou ao supermercado.

Use standard contractions where natural Portuguese requires them.

---

## 8. Ser and Estar

Do not teach the oversimplified rule:

> ser = permanent  
> estar = temporary

Teach them through recurring usage patterns.

### Common uses of `ser`

Often used for:

- identity,
- origin,
- profession,
- classification,
- time,
- date.

Examples:

> Sou português.

> Ela é médica.

> Hoje é segunda-feira.

### Common uses of `estar`

Often used for:

- states,
- physical condition,
- emotional condition,
- location in many ordinary contexts.

Examples:

> Estou cansado.

> Ela está em casa.

Introduce finer distinctions when relevant to the current material.

---

## 9. Ter

Use `ter` naturally for:

- possession,
- age,
- common idiomatic expressions.

Examples:

> Tenho um carro.

> Tenho vinte anos.

Avoid importing copular age constructions from other languages.

---

## 10. Progressive Aspect

European Portuguese commonly expresses an ongoing action with:

**estar a + infinitive**

Examples:

> Estou a estudar.

> Ela está a trabalhar.

> Estamos a falar português.

Use this as the default progressive pattern for PT-PT.

---

## 11. Reflexive and Pronominal Verbs

Teach reflexive and pronominal verbs as complete lexical patterns when useful.

Examples:

> chamar-se

> levantar-se

> sentar-se

> lembrar-se

Ensure that both verb conjugation and pronoun placement are correct.

Useful chunks include:

> Chamo-me Ana.

> Lembro-me disso.

---

## 12. Clitic Pronouns

Common clitic pronouns include:

- me
- te
- se
- o / a
- os / as
- lhe / lhes
- nos
- vos

European Portuguese clitic placement differs significantly from common Brazilian
usage and must be modeled consistently.

---

## 13. Enclisis

In many affirmative independent clauses, the clitic follows the verb.

This is **enclisis**.

Examples:

> Chamo-me João.

> Lembro-me disso.

> Senta-te aqui.

> Diz-lhe a verdade.

> Ele chama-se Pedro.

Treat enclisis as an important neutral PT-PT pattern, but not as an absolute
rule.

---

## 14. Proclisis

Certain syntactic environments place the clitic before the verb.

This is **proclisis**.

Common triggers include:

- negation,
- interrogative operators,
- relative elements,
- subordinate clauses,
- certain adverbs,
- certain quantifying expressions.

Examples:

> Não me lembro.

> Como te chamas?

> Quem te disse isso?

> O livro que me deste é interessante.

> Quando te vejo, digo-te.

Teach recurring patterns rather than presenting an exhaustive trigger list when
that level of detail is unnecessary.

---

## 15. Questions Do Not Automatically Trigger Proclisis

Do not teach that every question requires the clitic before the verb.

Compare:

> Como te chamas?

with:

> Lembras-te dele?

The first contains an interrogative element that triggers proclisis.

A yes/no question does not automatically do so.

---

## 16. Negation

Negation commonly triggers proclisis.

Examples:

> Não me lembro.

> Não te conheço.

> Nunca lhe disse isso.

A useful pattern is:

**negative trigger + clitic + verb**

---

## 17. Word Order

A common neutral order is:

**Subject + Verb + Object**

However, Portuguese allows variation for:

- emphasis,
- topicalization,
- clitic placement,
- adverbs,
- questions,
- discourse structure.

Use natural PT-PT word order rather than mechanically copying another language.

---

## 18. Adjective Position

Portuguese adjectives may appear before or after nouns depending on:

- meaning,
- lexical usage,
- register,
- style,
- emphasis.

Do not teach:

> adjectives always come after nouns

as an absolute rule.

Examples such as:

> um amigo velho

and

> um velho amigo

may differ in interpretation or nuance.

Introduce such distinctions only when useful.

---

## 19. Forms of Address

Forms of address depend strongly on:

- familiarity,
- age,
- social relationship,
- register,
- context.

Common PT-PT possibilities include:

- tu
- você
- o senhor
- a senhora
- the person's name
- title-based forms

Do not treat `você` as a simple universal equivalent of English `you`.

In Portugal, its social effect may vary by context.

---

## 20. Vocabulary Variety

Use common European Portuguese vocabulary by default.

Examples of frequent PT-PT forms include:

- telemóvel
- comboio
- autocarro
- casa de banho
- sumo

Do not replace these silently with Brazilian equivalents.

Regional comparison should be introduced only when pedagogically useful.

---

## 21. PT-PT vs PT-BR

Do not describe valid Brazilian Portuguese simply as "wrong."

When a relevant distinction appears:

1. identify the form or pattern as PT-BR when appropriate,
2. explain the difference briefly,
3. provide the natural PT-PT equivalent.

Example:

PT-BR:

> Estou estudando português.

PT-PT:

> Estou a estudar português.

The goal is consistent target-variety learning, not rejection of another valid
Portuguese variety.

---

## 22. Regional Drift Guardrail

Before producing corrections or examples, check that the output remains
consistent with PT-PT.

Watch especially for drift in:

- progressive constructions,
- clitic placement,
- vocabulary,
- forms of address,
- pronoun usage,
- article usage,
- infinitive constructions,
- pronunciation guidance.

Avoid mixing PT-PT grammar with clearly PT-BR vocabulary or vice versa unless
explicit comparison is the purpose.

---

## 23. Pronunciation Variety

Pronunciation guidance should target European Portuguese.

Do not default to Brazilian Portuguese pronunciation.

Where pronunciation varies within Portugal, prioritize:

1. intelligibility,
2. common standard usage,
3. awareness of legitimate variation.

Do not insist that one regional realization is the only correct pronunciation.

---

## 24. Unstressed Vowel Reduction

European Portuguese frequently reduces unstressed vowels.

This is one of the main reasons spoken PT-PT may sound substantially different
from its written form.

Learners should learn to recognize reduced vowels without altering standard
spelling.

---

## 25. Stress

Stress strongly affects pronunciation and vowel quality.

When useful:

- indicate the stressed syllable,
- contrast easily confused forms,
- correct repeated stress errors.

Do not overload basic explanations with unnecessary phonological theory.

---

## 26. Final Unstressed Vowels

Final unstressed vowels may be weak or strongly reduced in European Portuguese.

Learners should prioritize:

- listening recognition,
- natural rhythm,
- avoiding overly strong final vowels.

Precise phonetic detail may be introduced when needed.

---

## 27. Nasal Vowels

Portuguese nasal vowels and diphthongs require explicit attention.

Important orthographic patterns include:

- `ão`,
- vowel + `m`,
- vowel + `n` in relevant environments.

Teach them through real words and contrasts rather than isolated abstract
descriptions.

---

## 28. `lh`

`lh` represents a palatal sound.

Teach it as a single sound pattern rather than independent `l` + `h`.

Use real lexical examples when pronunciation practice is required.

---

## 29. `nh`

`nh` represents a palatal nasal sound.

Teach it as a unified sound pattern rather than independent `n` + `h`.

---

## 30. `r`

Portuguese `r` varies according to:

- position,
- speaker,
- dialect,
- phonetic environment.

Do not insist on one realization as the only valid PT-PT pronunciation.

Prioritize recognition and intelligibility.

---

## 31. `s` and `z`

The pronunciation of written `s` varies according to phonological context.

Do not teach one single pronunciation for every `s`.

Use actual words and connected speech when explaining these patterns.

---

## 32. Connected Speech

Natural European Portuguese commonly includes:

- vowel reduction,
- weak unstressed syllables,
- linking,
- resyllabification,
- reduced function words,
- compressed rhythm.

Spoken PT-PT may therefore sound significantly faster or less transparent than
carefully pronounced textbook language.

Train recognition gradually.

---

## 33. Written Form vs Spoken Form

Clearly distinguish between:

1. standard written form,
2. ordinary spoken pronunciation,
3. strongly reduced colloquial pronunciation.

Do not write reduced spoken forms as though they were standard orthography.

---

## 34. IPA

Use IPA when:

- the learner requests it,
- a sound distinction requires precision,
- it materially improves pronunciation guidance.

For many learners, especially beginners, plain-language explanation and example
words may be more useful than dense IPA transcription.

---

## 35. Orthography

Use current standard Portuguese orthography unless the source intentionally uses
an older spelling.

If older orthography appears:

1. preserve it when quoting,
2. identify it when relevant,
3. provide the modern form separately when useful.

Do not silently alter quotations.

---

## 36. Source Fidelity

When working from textbooks or user-provided language:

- preserve the original wording,
- preserve the original regional variety,
- do not fabricate missing dialogue,
- distinguish source material from generated examples.

If a source sentence appears unusual, explain it rather than silently rewriting
it.

Detailed source-handling behavior is governed by:

`shared/policies/source-integrity.md`

---

## 37. Generated Examples

Generated examples should:

- sound natural in Portugal,
- match the requested learning level,
- reinforce the target structure,
- avoid unnecessary complexity,
- remain regionally consistent.

Do not present generated examples as textbook quotations.

---

## 38. Avoid False Absolutes

Avoid oversimplified claims such as:

- all questions use proclisis,
- `ser` means permanent,
- `estar` means temporary,
- adjectives always follow nouns,
- `você` is always neutral,
- every noun ending in `-a` is feminine,
- subject pronouns must always be expressed.

Prefer wording such as:

- usually,
- commonly,
- in this structure,
- in neutral contexts,
- in European Portuguese,
- in this example.

---

## 39. Naturalness vs Correctness

Distinguish between:

- grammatically incorrect,
- non-standard,
- understandable but unusual,
- grammatically valid but less natural,
- natural PT-PT usage.

Do not label a valid but less idiomatic expression simply as wrong.

When appropriate, explain:

> 這個形式文法上可以成立，但在歐洲葡萄牙語中更自然的是……

---

## 40. Core Principle

Teach Portuguese as it is naturally used in Portugal.

Prefer:

**natural PT-PT + consistent regional usage + clear reusable patterns**

over:

**excessive theory + mixed varieties + false absolute rules**
