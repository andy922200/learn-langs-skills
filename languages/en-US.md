# American English (en-US)

Shared language reference for **American English (en-US)**.

This file defines stable grammar, usage, pronunciation, and regional conventions
for American English.

It is level-independent and may be reused across CEFR levels and learning
directions.

General teaching behavior belongs in `shared/pedagogy/`.

CEFR difficulty and scaffolding belong in `shared/cefr/`.

Cross-language comparison belongs in `language-pairs/`.

---

## 1. Default Variety

Always treat **American English (en-US)** as the target variety.

Unless another variety is explicitly requested, generated language should follow
natural usage in the United States.

Prefer:

- en-US spelling,
- en-US vocabulary,
- en-US punctuation and formatting conventions,
- en-US date, number, and unit conventions,
- en-US pronunciation guidance.

Do not silently drift toward British English or another variety.

Most English grammar is shared across varieties. Variety differences matter
mainly in spelling, vocabulary, a small set of grammatical preferences,
punctuation, and pronunciation.

---

## 2. Subject Required

English is not a pro-drop language.

A finite clause normally needs an overt subject.

Natural:

> It's raining.

> There is a problem.

Not natural:

> *Is raining.

Use dummy subjects `it` and `there` when the sentence has no real subject:

> It's important to sleep.

> There are three options.

Imperatives are the main regular exception:

> Close the door.

---

## 3. Articles

English uses the definite article `the` and the indefinite articles `a` / `an`.

A / an depends on the sound that follows, not the spelling:

> a university, an hour

Use:

- `a / an` for a singular countable noun that is new or non-specific,
- `the` for something specific or already identified, or unique,
- no article for plural or uncountable nouns used in a general sense.

> I need a pen.

> The pen on the table is mine.

> Pens are useful.

> Water is essential.

Article choice depends on context, not on a single rule. Teach it through
examples rather than a full theory.

---

## 4. Countability and Plurals

English distinguishes countable and uncountable nouns.

Countable nouns have singular and plural forms:

> one book, two books

Regular plurals add `-s` / `-es`. Some plurals are irregular:

> child → children, person → people, man → men

Some nouns are uncountable in standard usage and are not pluralized:

> information, advice, research, evidence, equipment, furniture, homework

Use a quantity expression instead of a plural form:

> a piece of advice

> a lot of information

Some nouns have both countable and uncountable uses with a difference in meaning:

> a paper / paper, a work / work, an experience / experience

Do not present uncountability as always logical. Teach common nouns as
collocations.

---

## 5. Subject–Verb Agreement

Present tense verbs take `-s` with a third-person singular subject:

> She works. They work.

`be` and `have` are irregular:

> I am, you are, he is; I have, she has

Do not omit agreement because the meaning is clear.

Collective nouns (`team`, `family`, `government`) usually take a singular verb in
American English:

> The team is ready.

Agreement follows the head noun, not the noun closest to the verb:

> The list of names is long.

---

## 6. Tense and Aspect

English marks tense and aspect in the verb phrase.

Core forms include:

- simple present, simple past,
- present continuous, past continuous,
- present perfect, past perfect,
- future with `will`, `be going to`, and present continuous.

Key contrasts:

> I live in Taipei. (a general fact)

> I'm living in Taipei this year. (a temporary situation)

> I have lived here for five years. (still true now)

> I lived there for five years. (finished)

Stative verbs (`know`, `like`, `want`, `believe`) are normally not used in the
continuous form for their basic meanings.

Tense should be consistent within a passage unless the time reference changes.

---

## 7. Present Perfect in American English

The present perfect exists in American English and is used for a past event with
present relevance.

Compared with British English, American English more readily uses the simple
past with `already`, `yet`, and `just` in informal usage:

> Did you eat yet?

> I already ate.

Both forms are acceptable in informal American English.

In formal writing, prefer the standard present perfect where the sense of
present relevance matters:

> Sales have increased since 2020.

The past participle of `get` is normally `gotten` in American English:

> She has gotten better.

---

## 8. Modals

Common modals include `can`, `could`, `may`, `might`, `will`, `would`,
`should`, `must`.

Modals are followed by the base form without `to`:

> She can swim.

> You should go.

Modals have multiple functions (ability, permission, possibility, obligation,
politeness). The function depends on context.

`Shall` is rare in everyday American English, except in fixed offers such as
`Shall we go?`

Prefer:

> Would you mind…? / Could you…?

over overly direct requests when politeness is needed.

---

## 9. Word Order

Declarative word order is fixed: subject – verb – object.

> The author supports the proposal.

Adverbs have some flexibility, but the order between verb and object is fixed:

> I usually read the news in the morning.

Not natural:

> *I read usually the news.

Place and time expressions normally follow the verb phrase, with place before
time:

> I study at the library every day.

Word order in embedded questions follows statement order:

> I wonder where she lives.

---

## 10. Questions and Negation

Yes/no questions and most wh-questions use inversion or `do`-support:

> Do you live here?

> Where does she work?

Do not use `do` with `be` as the main verb or with modals:

> Are you ready? Can you help?

Negation uses `not` with an auxiliary, or `do`-support:

> I do not (don't) know. She is not (isn't) here. He cannot (can't) come.

Double negatives are not standard:

> *I don't have no time.

Use `any` after negation:

> I don't have any time.

---

## 11. Relative Clauses

Relative clauses follow the noun.

> the book that I bought yesterday

> the person who called me

Commas mark non-restrictive clauses:

> My sister, who lives in Boston, is a nurse.

In formal American style, `that` is often preferred for restrictive clauses and
`which` for non-restrictive clauses. Treat this as a style convention, not a
strict rule.

The relative pronoun may be omitted when it is the object:

> the book I bought

---

## 12. Prepositions and Collocations

English relies heavily on prepositions.

Prepositions are often fixed with particular words and cannot be predicted from
meaning alone:

> depend on, interested in, good at, afraid of

Teach high-frequency combinations as chunks.

Time and place patterns include:

> at 5 p.m., on Monday, in July

> at the station, on the table, in the room

Do not present a preposition as having one universal equivalent in another
language.

---

## 13. Phrasal Verbs

Many English verbs combine with a particle to form a phrasal verb.

> turn on, give up, look for, figure out

The meaning is often not literal.

Some phrasal verbs are separable:

> turn the light on / turn on the light

A pronoun object normally goes between verb and particle:

> turn it on

Teach phrasal verbs in context, not as isolated lists. Prefer common phrasal
verbs before rare ones.

---

## 14. Connectors and Sentence Boundaries

English clauses need an appropriate connector or punctuation.

Avoid joining two independent clauses with only a comma (a comma splice):

> *The plan is good, it is too expensive.

Use a period, a semicolon, or a connector:

> The plan is good, but it is too expensive.

Paired connectors such as `although` / `but` are not used together:

> Although it was raining, we went out.

Not standard:

> *Although it was raining, but we went out.

The same applies to `because` / `so`.

---

## 15. Register and Style

English has a range of registers from informal spoken to formal academic.

Contractions (`don't`, `it's`) are normal in speech and informal writing and are
usually reduced in formal academic writing.

Stance can be adjusted with hedging:

> may, might, tend to, seems to, arguably, it is likely that

Academic and argumentative writing usually states a position clearly and
supports it with reasons and evidence.

Do not assume a single "correct" register. Choose it according to task and
audience.

---

## 16. Forms of Address

English has one second-person pronoun, `you`. It does not have a formal/informal
pronoun distinction.

Politeness is expressed through:

- modal verbs and indirect requests,
- titles and names,
- tone and word choice.

First names are common in many American settings, including workplaces.

Titles include `Mr.`, `Ms.`, `Mrs.`, `Dr.`, and `Professor`. Use `Ms.` for a
woman when her preference is unknown.

`Sir` and `ma'am` are common in service contexts and in some regions.

Singular `they` is standard for a person whose gender is unknown or who uses
`they`.

## 19. Grammar and Usage Variety

Some grammatical preferences differ by variety:

- `gotten` (en-US) vs `got` (en-GB) as the participle of `get`,
- `on the weekend` vs `at the weekend`,
- `Monday through Friday` vs `Monday to Friday`,
- `different from / than` vs `different from / to`,
- `Do you have…?` vs `Have you got…?`,
- `learned / spelled` vs `learnt / spelt`,
- bare subjunctive after some verbs: `It is important that he be on time`.

Do not treat a variety difference as an error unless the task requires a
particular variety.

---

## 20. Punctuation, Dates, Numbers, and Units

American conventions include:

- periods and commas inside closing quotation marks,
- double quotation marks as the primary marks,
- date order `month/day/year` (for example `9/30/2026`),
- 12-hour time with `a.m.` / `p.m.`,
- decimal point (`3.5`) and comma for thousands (`1,000`),
- imperial units in everyday use (miles, pounds, Fahrenheit).

The serial (Oxford) comma is common but not universal. Be consistent.

State the date format when ambiguity could cause confusion.

---

## 21. Pronunciation Variety

Use **General American** as the default pronunciation reference.

General American is a convenient teaching reference, not the only American
accent. Do not present it as the only correct pronunciation.

Key features include:

- rhotic `r` (`r` is pronounced after a vowel: `car`, `hard`),
- flapping of intervocalic `t` / `d` (`water`, `better`),
- reduced vowels in unstressed syllables,
- strong stress patterns.

Do not imply that spelling reliably predicts pronunciation.

---

## 22. Stress, Rhythm, and Reduction

English is often described as stress-timed.

Content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) are usually stressed. Function
words (`a`, `the`, `of`, `to`) are usually reduced.

Word stress can change meaning or word class:

> REcord (noun) / reCORD (verb)

The most common reduced vowel is the schwa `/ə/`.

Natural speech includes linking, reduction, and contractions:

> want to → "wanna", going to → "gonna"

Teach such forms for recognition. Do not require them for production unless the
learner wants them.

---

## 23. Sounds Often Needing Attention

Sounds that are often difficult for learners from many first languages include:

- `/θ/` and `/ð/` (`think`, `this`),
- `/v/` and `/w/`,
- `/r/` and `/l/`,
- vowel contrast such as `/ɪ/` and `/i/` (`ship`, `sheep`),
- final consonants and consonant clusters (`asked`, `texts`).

Use audio, articulation description, and minimal pairs. Use IPA only when it
helps.

Avoid transliterating English sounds with characters from another writing system.

---

## 24. Source Fidelity

When learning material is supplied by the learner:

- preserve the source text exactly,
- do not silently correct or change the variety,
- distinguish the source from tutor-generated content.

If the source uses British spelling or vocabulary, preserve it and note the
variety difference only as much as needed.

Follow `shared/policies/source-integrity.md`.

---

## 25. Generated Examples

Generated examples should:

- be natural American English,
- match the learner's level,
- use current, ordinary usage,
- avoid invented facts, quotations, or sources presented as real.

Do not present tutor-generated text as if it came from a real publication.

Use a small number of clear examples rather than many similar ones.

---

## 26. Avoid False Absolutes

Do not state rules more strongly than English usage supports.

Avoid:

> Adjectives never come after a noun.

> Never end a sentence with a preposition.

> That is always used for restrictive clauses.

Prefer:

> Usually…

> In formal writing, …

> Many writers prefer…

When usage varies by register or variety, say so briefly.

---

## 27. Naturalness vs Correctness

A sentence may be grammatical and still sound unnatural.

Distinguish:

- ungrammatical,
- grammatical but unnatural,
- natural but informal,
- natural in another variety.

When giving feedback, say which kind of issue it is. Do not label every
unnatural sentence as wrong.

Where useful, offer a more natural alternative and explain the difference in one
sentence.

---

## 28. Core Principle

Treat American English as a living variety used across many registers.

Prefer:

**natural en-US + clear explanation + honest note about variation**

over:

**rigid rule-giving + false absolutes + silent variety drift**
