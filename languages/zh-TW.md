# Traditional Chinese — Taiwan (zh-TW)

Shared language reference for **Traditional Chinese as used in Taiwan (`zh-TW`)**.

This file defines stable grammar, vocabulary, writing, pronunciation, register,
and regional conventions for Taiwan Mandarin and written Traditional Chinese.

It is level-independent and may be reused across CEFR-like learning levels and
different learning directions.

General teaching behavior belongs in `shared/pedagogy/`.

Difficulty and scaffolding belong in level policies.

Cross-language comparison belongs in `language-pairs/`.

---

## 1. Default Variety

Always treat **Traditional Chinese as used in Taiwan (`zh-TW`)** as the target
variety.

Unless another variety is explicitly requested, generated Chinese should follow
natural contemporary usage in Taiwan.

Prefer:

- Traditional Chinese characters,
- Taiwan vocabulary,
- Taiwan Mandarin usage,
- Taiwan punctuation conventions,
- Taiwan forms of address,
- Taiwan educational terminology,
- Taiwan pronunciation guidance.

Do not silently drift toward Simplified Chinese or Mainland China-specific
wording.

---

## 2. Writing System

Use **Traditional Chinese characters** by default.

Examples:

> 學習

> 語言

> 電腦

> 資訊

> 軟體

Do not silently convert to Simplified Chinese forms such as:

> 学习

> 语言

> 电脑

> 信息

> 软件

unless explicitly requested.

---

## 4. Taiwan Usage Guardrail

Before producing Chinese examples, check for unintended regional drift.

Watch especially for:

- Mainland China vocabulary,
- Simplified Chinese characters,
- Mainland-specific institutional terminology,
- unnatural direct translations,
- regionally mismatched internet terminology,
- non-Taiwan educational terminology.

Prefer natural Taiwanese usage when several valid Chinese forms exist.

---

## 5. Mandarin as the Default Spoken Variety

When pronunciation is discussed, use **Taiwan Mandarin** as the default spoken
reference.

Do not assume Beijing Mandarin pronunciation or vocabulary is the only standard.

Where Taiwan Mandarin varies naturally among speakers, prioritize:

1. intelligibility,
2. common contemporary Taiwan usage,
3. awareness of legitimate variation.

---

## 6. Zhuyin and Pinyin

For pronunciation guidance, **Bopomofo / Zhuyin (注音符號)** may be used as the
primary phonetic aid for Taiwan-oriented learners.

Example:

> 學：ㄒㄩㄝˊ

Hanyu Pinyin may also be provided when useful:

> xué

Do not assume Pinyin must replace Zhuyin in a Taiwan-focused learning context.

When both are useful, they may be shown together.

---

## 7. Tones

Mandarin uses lexical tone.

The standard tone categories are:

- 第一聲
- 第二聲
- 第三聲
- 第四聲
- 輕聲

Tone should be taught as part of the word rather than as an optional
pronunciation detail.

A tone error may change meaning or reduce intelligibility.

---

## 8. Tone Sandhi

Tone pronunciation may change in connected speech.

Important examples include:

- third-tone sandhi,
- `一` tone changes,
- `不` tone changes.

Example:

> 你好

is commonly pronounced with tone sandhi rather than as two isolated full third
tones.

Do not confuse underlying lexical tone with surface pronunciation.

---

## 9. Retroflex and Non-Retroflex Variation

Taiwan Mandarin may show less strongly retroflex pronunciation than some
northern Mandarin varieties.

Sounds written in Pinyin as:

- zh
- ch
- sh
- r

may vary naturally among Taiwan speakers.

Do not mark every less-retroflex realization as incorrect if it remains natural
and intelligible in Taiwan Mandarin.

---

## 10. `兒化`

Erhua (`兒化`) is much less central to ordinary Taiwan Mandarin than to some
northern Mainland varieties.

Do not introduce extensive `兒化` as a default Taiwan Mandarin feature.

If encountered in external material, explain it as a regional pronunciation and
morphological feature.

---

## 11. Word Order

A common basic word order is:

**Subject + Verb + Object**

Example:

> 我喝咖啡。

However, Chinese word order is strongly affected by:

- topic-comment structure,
- time expressions,
- location phrases,
- aspect,
- information structure,
- discourse context.

Do not reduce Chinese grammar to SVO alone.

---

## 12. Topic–Comment Structure

Chinese frequently uses topic-comment organization.

Example:

> 這本書，我很喜歡。

The first phrase establishes the topic, and the remainder comments on it.

Do not assume every topic must be the grammatical subject.

This distinction becomes important in contrastive language teaching.

---

## 13. Subject Omission

Chinese frequently omits subjects when context makes them recoverable.

Example:

> 吃過了嗎？

The subject may be omitted because it is understood from context.

Do not force explicit pronouns into every sentence.

Natural Chinese often relies heavily on discourse context.

---

## 14. Pronouns

Common personal pronouns include:

- 我
- 你
- 您
- 他
- 她
- 它
- 我們
- 你們
- 他們
- 她們

Spoken Mandarin does not distinguish:

> 他 / 她 / 它

phonologically; all are pronounced `tā`.

Their distinction is primarily written.

---

## 15. `您`

`您` is a respectful second-person pronoun.

Its use depends on:

- age,
- hierarchy,
- service context,
- politeness,
- relationship.

Do not map it mechanically to every formal second-person form in another
language.

Taiwan usage may often prefer:

- title,
- role name,
- surname + title,
- omitted pronoun,

depending on context.

---

## 16. No Grammatical Gender

Modern Mandarin does not have grammatical gender comparable to languages such
as Portuguese, German, or Spanish.

Nouns and adjectives do not normally inflect for masculine/feminine agreement.

Do not confuse written pronouns such as:

> 他 / 她

with grammatical gender systems.

---

## 17. No Verb Conjugation for Person

Chinese verbs generally do not change form according to grammatical person.

Example:

> 我住在台北。

> 你住在台北。

> 他住在台北。

The verb:

> 住

remains unchanged.

Person is expressed through context, pronouns, nouns, or discourse.

---

## 18. Tense

Chinese does not encode tense through verb conjugation in the same way as many
European languages.

Time is commonly expressed through:

- context,
- time expressions,
- aspect markers,
- particles,
- auxiliary verbs.

Examples:

> 我昨天去台北。

> 我明天去台北。

The verb `去` itself does not change form.

Do not teach Chinese as "having no time reference."

It expresses temporal relationships differently.

---

## 19. Aspect

Aspect is central to Chinese grammar.

Important markers include:

- 了
- 過
- 著

These should not be mapped directly onto European tense labels.

### `了`

May indicate completion, change of state, or other discourse functions depending
on context.

Example:

> 我吃飯了。

### `過`

Often indicates experiential aspect.

Example:

> 我去過日本。

### `著`

May indicate a continuing state or manner.

Example:

> 門開著。

Teach these through usage patterns rather than one-word translation rules.

---

## 20. `了` Is Not Simply Past Tense

Do not teach:

> 了 = past tense

This is inaccurate.

Compare:

> 下雨了。

This describes a change of state rather than simply a past event.

The interpretation of `了` depends on syntax and discourse context.

---

## 21. Copular `是`

`是` is commonly used to link noun phrases or identify/classify something.

Example:

> 我是學生。

However, Chinese does not use `是` before ordinary adjectival predicates in the
same way English uses `be`.

Natural:

> 我很累。

Not normally:

> *我是很累。

Do not insert `是` mechanically based on another language's copular grammar.

---

## 22. Adjectival Predicates

Chinese adjectives can function predicatively without a separate copular verb.

Example:

> 天氣很好。

> 我很忙。

`很` does not always carry a strong literal meaning equivalent to "very."

In many neutral adjectival predicates, it can serve an important grammatical or
prosodic role.

Avoid teaching:

> 很 = always "very"

as an absolute rule.

---

## 23. Negation

Common negators include:

- 不
- 沒
- 沒有

Their distribution differs.

### `不`

Often used for:

- habitual negation,
- present/future negation,
- states,
- volitional verbs.

Example:

> 我不喝咖啡。

### `沒 / 沒有`

Often used for:

- non-occurrence,
- non-completion,
- possession negation.

Example:

> 我沒有去。

> 我沒有車。

Do not teach `不` and `沒` as interchangeable equivalents of "not."

---

## 24. Questions

Chinese questions may be formed through several strategies.

### Sentence-final `嗎`

> 你是學生嗎？

### Interrogative words

> 你住哪裡？

> 你叫什麼名字？

### A-not-A

> 你要不要去？

> 你是不是學生？

Interrogative words usually remain in the position occupied by the answer.

Do not automatically move question words to the beginning as in many European
languages.

---

## 25. Question Words In Situ

Compare:

> 你住哪裡？

The question word `哪裡` remains where a location phrase would normally appear.

Answer:

> 我住台北。

This is a major structural feature of Mandarin questions.

---

## 26. Measure Words and Classifiers

Chinese frequently uses classifiers between numerals/demonstratives and nouns.

Examples:

> 一個人

> 一本書

> 一杯咖啡

> 一張票

> 一隻狗

Do not default to `個` for every noun when a common specific classifier exists.

However, `個` is widespread and may be acceptable in many colloquial contexts.

---

## 27. `的`

`的` has several important uses, including possession and nominal modification.

Examples:

> 我的書

> 漂亮的房子

> 我昨天買的書

Do not treat `的` as a simple equivalent of English `'s` or Portuguese `de`.

Its usage depends on the construction.

---

## 28. Relative Clauses

Chinese relative clauses typically appear before the noun and use `的`.

Example:

> 我昨天買的書

Structure:

> [我昨天買的] + 書

This differs substantially from many European languages where relative clauses
follow the noun.

---

## 29. Time Expressions

Time expressions frequently appear before the main verb.

Example:

> 我明天去台北。

A common pattern is:

**Subject + Time + Verb + Object/Complement**

However, topic and discourse structure may allow variation.

---

## 30. Location

Location may be expressed with structures involving:

- 在
- 裡
- 上
- 下
- 前面
- 後面
- 旁邊

Examples:

> 我在台北。

> 書在桌上。

> 他在家裡。

Teach location expressions as complete constructions rather than isolated
preposition equivalents.

---

## 31. `在`

`在` may function in several ways.

Examples:

Location:

> 我在學校。

Location before an action:

> 我在學校上課。

Progressive marker:

> 我在吃飯。

Do not assume every `在` corresponds to the same grammatical category in another
language.

---

## 32. Progressive Constructions

Common progressive expressions include:

> 我在吃飯。

> 我正在吃飯。

`正在` may add emphasis to an action currently in progress.

Do not mechanically map these onto all uses of progressive aspect in European
languages.

---

## 33. Result Complements

Chinese frequently expresses the result of an action through verb complements.

Examples:

> 看懂

> 吃完

> 找到

> 寫好

These should often be learned as meaningful combinations rather than as simple
verb + adverb translations.

---

## 34. Directional Complements

Directional complements include forms such as:

- 上來
- 下去
- 進來
- 出去
- 回來

They may express literal motion or extended meanings.

Teach them through recurring constructions.

---

## 35. Serial Verb and Coverb-Like Structures

Chinese often expresses relationships through sequences involving verbs or
coverb-like elements.

Examples:

> 我跟朋友去吃飯。

> 我坐捷運去公司。

Do not impose European prepositional categories mechanically onto these
structures.

---

## 36. Plurality

Chinese nouns generally do not require plural morphology.

Example:

> 一本書

> 三本書

The noun `書` itself does not change.

`們` is used with certain human pronouns and nouns, but it is not a universal
plural suffix.

Examples:

> 我們

> 學生們

Do not add `們` mechanically to every plural noun.

---

## 37. Count and Mass Interpretation

Many Chinese nouns rely on classifiers, quantity expressions, and context rather
than obligatory singular/plural marking.

Teach noun quantity together with common classifiers and measure phrases.

---

## 38. Adverbs

Common adverbs often appear before the verb or adjective.

Examples:

> 我常常去。

> 他已經吃飯了。

> 我真的很喜歡。

Placement depends on the adverb and structure.

Avoid overgeneralizing one fixed position for all adverbs.

---

## 39. Sentence-Final Particles

Taiwan Mandarin frequently uses sentence-final particles such as:

- 啊
- 喔
- 哦
- 啦
- 呢
- 吧
- 嘛

These may express:

- attitude,
- softness,
- emphasis,
- suggestion,
- confirmation,
- discourse stance.

Do not translate them mechanically word-for-word.

Their pragmatic effect matters more than literal equivalence.

---

## 40. Taiwan Conversational Particles

Contemporary Taiwan Mandarin commonly uses conversational particles in natural
speech.

Examples:

> 好啊。

> 可以喔。

> 不用了啦。

> 真的嗎？

Use them when natural, but avoid overloading beginner examples with excessive
colloquial particles.

---

## 41. Politeness

Taiwan Mandarin often expresses politeness through:

- indirect phrasing,
- titles,
- honorific wording,
- softening particles,
- contextual omission,
- polite formulas.

Examples:

> 不好意思

> 麻煩你了

> 請問

> 謝謝

> 辛苦了

Do not assume politeness is encoded primarily through pronouns.

---

## 42. Forms of Address

Forms of address may include:

- surname + title,
- professional title,
- kinship-style terms,
- given name,
- full name,
- role nouns.

Examples:

> 林老師

> 王醫師

> 經理

> 阿姨

Choice depends on social context.

Do not map European first-name/title systems directly onto Taiwan usage.

---

## 43. Taiwan Professional Titles

Prefer Taiwan-standard professional terminology where applicable.

Examples:

> 醫師

rather than defaulting to regionally different terminology when discussing a
medical doctor professionally.

Institutional and occupational vocabulary should follow Taiwan conventions.

---

## 44. Punctuation

Use contemporary Traditional Chinese punctuation conventions.

Common punctuation includes:

- `，`
- `。`
- `？`
- `！`
- `：`
- `；`
- `「」`
- `『』`
- `（）`

For Chinese prose, prefer full-width Chinese punctuation.

---

## 45. Quotation Marks

Taiwan Traditional Chinese commonly uses:

> 「……」

for primary quotation marks.

Nested quotations may use:

> 『……』

Do not default to Mainland-style quotation marks when producing Taiwan-oriented
Chinese prose.

---

## 46. Numbers

Arabic numerals and Chinese numerals are both common.

Use according to context.

Examples:

> 3 個人

> 三個人

> 2026 年

> 九月

Do not enforce one representation universally.

---

## 47. Dates

Taiwan Chinese commonly writes dates in year–month–day order.

Example:

> 2026 年 9 月 30 日

Short forms may also appear:

> 2026/09/30

Use context-appropriate formatting.

---

## 48. Time

Common expressions include:

> 上午九點

> 下午三點半

> 晚上七點

Taiwan commonly uses both 12-hour daypart expressions and 24-hour digital time
depending on context.

---

## 49. Taiwan Calendar Context

Gregorian dates should be the default unless the context specifically requires
the Republic of China calendar.

Example:

> 2026 年

The ROC calendar may appear in official Taiwan contexts:

> 民國 115 年

Do not convert automatically unless useful or requested.

---

## 50. Pronunciation vs Writing

Do not treat written Chinese characters as direct pronunciation guides.

Pronunciation should be provided separately using:

- Zhuyin,
- Pinyin,
- tone information,
- audio when available.

Different characters may share the same pronunciation.

---

## 51. Homophones

Mandarin contains many homophones.

Context and characters distinguish meanings.

For vocabulary learning, prefer teaching:

**character + pronunciation + meaning + context**

rather than pronunciation alone.

---

## 52. Character Learning

When Chinese is the target language, characters should be introduced as part of
meaningful vocabulary.

Useful information may include:

- Traditional character,
- pronunciation,
- meaning,
- common word,
- useful component or radical when pedagogically helpful.

Avoid turning every character into a detailed etymology lesson.

---

## 53. Radicals and Components

Radicals and character components may help recognition and memory.

Use them when they provide a useful pattern.

Do not imply that every component transparently predicts modern meaning or
pronunciation.

---

## 54. Handwriting vs Recognition

Recognizing a character and writing it from memory are different skills.

A learner may be able to:

- recognize,
- type,
- read aloud,

before being able to handwrite accurately.

Treat these as separate learning outcomes when relevant.

---

## 55. Typing

For Taiwan-oriented learners, common input methods may include:

- Zhuyin input,
- Cangjie,
- Dayi,
- Pinyin-based input,
- handwriting input.

Do not assume Pinyin input is the default in Taiwan.

---

## 56. Register

Distinguish between:

- formal written Chinese,
- neutral written Chinese,
- conversational Taiwan Mandarin,
- highly colloquial speech,
- internet slang.

Do not use highly colloquial or slang-heavy language unless appropriate to the
task.

---

## 57. Formal Written Chinese

Formal written Chinese may differ noticeably from natural conversation.

For learners, label strongly formal vocabulary or constructions when needed.

Do not treat formal written usage as the default model for everyday speech.

---

## 58. Spoken Taiwan Mandarin

Natural spoken Taiwan Mandarin may include:

- omitted subjects,
- discourse particles,
- contractions,
- repetition,
- fillers,
- topic-comment structures.

Generated dialogue should sound natural without becoming unnecessarily slangy.

---

## 59. Code-Switching and Borrowings

Taiwan Mandarin may include borrowings or code-switching, especially in:

- technology,
- business,
- pop culture,
- everyday speech.

Use standard Taiwan Chinese terminology by default.

Introduce English borrowings only when they are naturally used in the relevant
context.

---

## 60. Source Fidelity

When working from textbooks or learner-provided Chinese:

- preserve the original script,
- preserve the source variety,
- do not silently convert Simplified ↔ Traditional,
- do not silently regionalize vocabulary,
- do not fabricate missing content.

If adaptation to Taiwan usage is useful, present it separately.

Example:

> 原文：
> ...
>
> 台灣較常見的說法：
> ...

Follow:

`shared/policies/source-integrity.md`

---

## 61. Simplified Chinese Input

If source material is written in Simplified Chinese:

1. preserve it when quoting,
2. identify the source script if relevant,
3. provide a Taiwan Traditional Chinese equivalent when useful.

Do not silently pretend that the original source used Traditional Chinese.

---

## 62. Regional Vocabulary Comparison

When another Mandarin variety uses a different term, distinguish:

- valid regional usage,
- Taiwan-preferred usage.

Do not describe another region's valid vocabulary simply as grammatically wrong.

The goal is consistency with the selected target variety.

---

## 63. Naturalness vs Correctness

Distinguish between:

- grammatically incorrect,
- understandable but unnatural,
- regionally mismatched,
- overly formal,
- overly colloquial,
- natural Taiwan usage.

Example feedback may say:

> 這句可以理解，但在台灣更自然的說法是……

Do not label every regional difference as an error.

---

## 64. Avoid False Absolutes

Avoid oversimplified claims such as:

- Chinese has no grammar.
- Chinese has no tense.
- `了` means past tense.
- `很` always means "very."
- every noun needs a classifier in every context.
- `們` is simply the universal plural marker.
- Chinese word order is always SVO.
- `是` equals English "be."
- Taiwan Mandarin pronunciation must match Beijing Mandarin.

Prefer precise wording such as:

- commonly,
- often,
- in this structure,
- in neutral conversation,
- in Taiwan usage,
- depending on context.

---

## 65. Generated Examples

Generated examples should:

- use Traditional Chinese characters,
- sound natural in Taiwan,
- match the learner's level,
- avoid unnecessary regional mixing,
- use appropriate register,
- reinforce the current learning target.

Do not present generated examples as quotations from source material.

---

## 66. Core Principle

Teach Chinese as it is naturally written and spoken in contemporary Taiwan.

Prefer:

**natural zh-TW + Traditional characters + Taiwan vocabulary + clear usage patterns**

over:

**literal translation + mixed regional standards + unnecessary prescriptivism**
